\begin{abstract}
% 决策 E2：内容隐私上界必须出现在摘要，不能埋进 §Limitations。
% 决策 D1：贡献陈述按"分析 x 系统各半"写 —— 分析在前，系统是分析所需的那一实例。
Proof-of-Policy (PoP) lets an auditor check that an LLM agent's response satisfies
a published policy, without trusted hardware and, in the certificate's public
values, without the operator disclosing the response in the clear. Its
zero-knowledge property is delivered by the \texttt{gr\-oth16} wrapper tier,
which we measured (1{,}874\,B, verified); the default \texttt{core} tier is
\emph{not} zero-knowledge, and neither tier prevents recovery of a low-entropy
response by re-execution. Our contribution is analytical before it is
constructive. We organise compliance certificates as a
binding--hiding--verifiability (BHV) framework in which one measurable
quantity---the guessing entropy of the bound secret given the adversary's side
information---governs the response side, a receiver-supplied freshness budget
governs the request side, and a policy commitment governs the policy side. A
conditional trilemma shows that, below the adversary's evaluation budget and
within the scheme class of \S\ref{sec:tradeoff} (guess-tight binding,
exact-recovery hiding, PV-2), at most two of binding, hiding and public
recomputability coexist (Proposition~\ref{thm:trilemma}). A leakage-profile
theorem separates public values, proof artefacts, and side information, and
explains why a witness-hiding proof closes one channel and not the others
(Theorem~\ref{thm:leak}). On the policy side we give a D-privacy definition
(Theorem~\ref{thm:dpriv}), a salted-commitment construction, and a measured
disclosure-level study: on a 20-variant synthetic corpus, policy-inference top-1
falls from $0.810$ at rule-name disclosure to $0.073$ at count-only; on a
300-candidate corpus the v1 certificate-layer L2 payload still recovers
$0.382$, because its unsalted evidence commitments link across samples; the
circuit-level v2 L2 salts them per response but has not been re-measured
(\S\ref{sec:eval-policy}). PoP is then presented as the instance these bounds
require---a policy compiler, a circuit whose witness-hiding tier is a wrapper
property, and a signed certificate layer---measured end to end.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Scope of privacy.} PoP provides \emph{public-value} policy
privacy: public values reveal neither the policy evaluation trace nor, in
policy-private mode, the policy text; the proof artefact is a separate channel,
closed only by the wrapper tier. The upper bound of the claim, used throughout,
is: (i) any publicly recomputable binding to $T$ admits a re-execution attack
whenever the adversary knows prompt and model, so $T$ is \emph{not}
unrecoverable (\S\ref{sec:binding}); (ii) the default \texttt{core} tier does not
hide its witness, so ``zero-knowledge'' names the \texttt{gr\-oth16} tier, and
that hiding claim is wrapper-level and not post-quantum
(\S\ref{sec:eval-proofs}); and (iii) a certificate without receiver-supplied
freshness is replayable without limit (Theorem~\ref{thm:t3}).
\S\ref{sec:disc-zkmap} states the condition under which the word applies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{L}{LM} agents increasingly take actions that carry
customer-facing consequences: replies, tool invocations, financial and medical
content. Regulation such as the EU AI Act (Articles~12 and~13) and enterprise
governance require that compliance be auditable. In practice it is taken on
trust: logs can be forged, guardrail claims cannot be checked, and content
privacy forbids retaining the plaintext that would settle the question.

Four lines of work each occupy one corner. \emph{TEE execution proofs}
(Proof-of-Guardrail and relatives) show that guardrail code ran and that inputs
and outputs were signed; as that work observes, \emph{execution is not
compliance}, and the approach carries latency and hardware dependencies.
\emph{TEE governance stacks} (Agentrust, cMCP, cA2A) inherit both. \emph{Formal
verification} (Lean-Agent and similar) requires the whole policy to be
formalised by hand and does not apply to black-box responses. \emph{ZK regex and
PII primitives} (zk-regex~V2, verifiable document redaction \cite{vdr}, ZK
Email) are building blocks, not policy-level systems.

The nearest work, WITNESS, supports two policy primitives, has no peer-reviewed
paper, and ships an Apache-2.0 reference implementation, so ``open source'' is
not a differentiator; it offers no content privacy. \texttt{@brivora/verify} is
not zero-knowledge, and DeepProve proves the \emph{model-computation} layer
rather than the \emph{policy-compliance} layer. In the systems we surveyed, we
did not find a general policy DSL compiled to ZK constraints, \emph{without} a
TEE, with selective disclosure of response content and a formal security model.
PoP occupies that slot---but the system is not the contribution we lead with.
Leading with a system invites the objection that a signature would do; the
analysis is what makes the system necessary, and it is what this paper is
organised around.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{What we do \emph{not} claim.} We do not claim that policy proofs
bind nothing: our system already binds the response through a verifier-issued
nonce (Definition~\ref{def:system}). We do not claim to be the first to observe
that a proof can fail to bind the artifact it describes: Hollow-LLM
\cite{hollowllm} formalises the \emph{effort gap} for ZK inference proofs, and
zk-attach \cite{zkattach} names the \emph{source-integrity gap} for predicate
proofs. Nor do we claim the separation theorem that our designated-verifier
interface makes tempting (``public verification is impossible when $k$ is
small''); Remark~\ref{rem:designated} gives the reason and
Corollary~\ref{cor:reexec} is the short version of it.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We isolate the gap that survives in every design we surveyed---\emph{divergent
  delivery}---and show that neither hardware nor any proof closes it, while a
  receiver contribution to the proven statement does
  (Proposition~\ref{thm:gap}).
  \item We give a binding--hiding--verifiability framework in which response,
  request and \emph{policy} are three instances of one accounting
  (\S\ref{sec:tradeoff}--\S\ref{sec:policy-side}). The single guess bound
  $q\cdot\mathsf{Guess}(X\mid Z)$ is standard; what is new is the framework, the
  conditional trilemma with its public-recomputation leg
  (Proposition~\ref{thm:trilemma}), the policy-side instance
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:dpriv}), the necessary conditions for composition and the
  weak form actually implemented (\S\ref{sec:disc-composition}), and the
  measurements.
  \item We price the fix. A certificate is replayable without limit unless the
  receiver contributes fresh randomness: with an empty nonce the
  \emph{replay/acceptance} advantage in $G_{\mathrm{rep}}$ is $1$ regardless of
  every other parameter, while under D2 the divergent-delivery advantage in
  $G_{\mathrm{del}}$ is only $\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:t3}).
  \item We describe PoP, a system that realises this design without a TEE
  (\S\ref{sec:system}), and report what its certificates leak about the policy
  they certify, including a measured inference curve over three disclosure
  levels (\S\ref{sec:eval-policy}).
\end{enumerate}

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{One spine, three sides.} Theorems~\ref{thm:t1}, \ref{thm:t3} and
Proposition~\ref{thm:trilemma} state the same fact about three different inputs:
\emph{a proof binds only as much as the committed, adversary-non-selectable
quantity has bits for}. The response side is governed by entropy,
$k = -\log_2 \mathsf{Guess}(T \mid Z)$ (Definition~\ref{def:k}); the request side
by freshness, $f$; the policy side by the guessing entropy $k_\pi$ of the policy
or by a $128$-bit salt that replaces it. None of the three can be bought with
more computation. We do not claim that PoP is ``stronger'' than TEE execution
proofs: the two prove orthogonal properties and compose rather than compete.

\section{System and Threat Model}
\label{sec:system}

The object of study is a \emph{policy-proof system}: a pipeline that compiles a
policy written in a small DSL into a circuit, evaluates an agent's response
against that circuit inside a zkVM, and emits a certificate binding the verdict
to the response. This section fixes the object and its adversary before any
theorem is stated.

\subsection{The system}
\label{sec:system-overview}

\paragraph{Pipeline and the cross-layer contract.} A policy package is compiled
to a \texttt{ConstraintSpec}---a JSON artefact carrying the compiled NFA---which
is the \emph{only} object crossing the layer boundary: the Python reference
implementation and the Rust/zkVM guest consume the same bytes, so \emph{spec}
drift is structurally impossible. Evaluator-semantics drift is not: the two
layers implement the decision separately, guarded by 19-vector cross-validation
plus differential fuzzing. The guest runs a deterministic evaluator and commits
its outcome. Seven of eight rule classes are decided \emph{inside} the circuit
(\texttt{keyword\_\allowbreak block},
\texttt{normalized\_\allowbreak keyword\_\allowbreak block},
\texttt{length\_\allowbreak bound}, \texttt{pattern\_\allowbreak block},
\texttt{format\_\allowbreak check}, \texttt{tool\_\allowbreak arg\_\allowbreak guard},
\texttt{budget\_\allowbreak bound}). The eighth,
\texttt{semantic\_bound}---``harm probability $\le$ threshold''---is precisely
the kind for which no formal proof can be learned, and the circuit does not
decide it: it registers only \emph{that the rule was delegated}, in the public
value \texttt{delegated}, and a verifier must \emph{conjunctively} check a
companion \texttt{ezkl}/halo2 proof. Verifying the zkVM side alone does not
establish compliance.

\paragraph{What the verdict is bound to.} Rules about the agent's \emph{tool
calls} cannot be judged against a list the prover fills in itself. Receipts are
therefore issued by a tool gateway after each call and chained (\texttt{seq},
\texttt{prev}, signature); the circuit checks the chain's \emph{structure} and
fails closed to \texttt{trace\_unbound}. Signature verification happens
\emph{off circuit} (Ed25519 inside the zkVM is prohibitively expensive), a
boundary we state rather than gloss: the circuit is blind to a forgery in the
chain's final element, and two independent off-circuit gates---digest comparison
and signature verification---carry that obligation. Tail truncation is closed by
a session-end \texttt{ToolSeal} commitment from the gateway, conditional on the
verifier holding the gateway's public key.

\paragraph{Dual privacy modes.} In \emph{transparent} mode the response and the
verdict are public. In \emph{private} mode the certificate publishes a response
commitment, a commitment per violation, and a redaction proof
(\texttt{redaction\_ok} together with \texttt{mask\_covered}). The guarantee is
that \emph{public values contain no plaintext}---not that $T$ cannot be
recovered. A commitment is a public function of $T$ against which anyone can
guess and check, while the response binding must be publicly recomputable for a
verifier to check the received string. Those two requirements are in direct
tension, and \S\ref{sec:binding} quantifies exactly when.

\paragraph{Certificates and anchoring.} A certificate is signed with a DSSE
envelope under Ed25519 with the key held by the issuing party, and its digest is
appended to a hash-chain anchor ledger. Two backends share one interface: a file
ledger, verifiable offline, and an on-chain registration storing only a 32-byte
digest. The transaction hash and on-chain timestamp are written back into the
local entry, so the offline chain and the public timestamp cross-check each
other and tampering with either is detectable.

\paragraph{Framework integration.} One gateway serves LangChain callbacks,
LangGraph (\texttt{guard\_node} plus full event streams), and MCP tool argument
and response checks, so trace certificates and proofs share a single chain of
evidence. The implementation runs 945 tests; 930 pass and 15 are skipped by
design, with no failures. The skipped set is enumerated in the repository's test
documentation (\texttt{docs/modules/08-tests-bench.md}).

\begin{definition}[Policy-proof system]
\label{def:system}
A policy-proof system is a tuple $(\mathsf{Prove}, \mathsf{Ver}, \pi, \mathcal{M})$.
On challenge $n$ from a verifier, $\mathsf{Prove}(\pi, T, n)$ outputs a
certificate $c$ whose public values include
\begin{multline*}
b \;=\; H(\texttt{pop-bind-v1} \,\|\, \mathsf{u32be}(|n|) \,\|\, n \,\|\, T),\\
C = H(T).
\end{multline*}
$\mathsf{Ver}(c, n, T')$ accepts iff the policy relation holds \emph{and}
$b$ recomputed from the received $T'$ matches.
\end{definition}

The relation does contain a term referring to the delivered bytes: $b$ is
recomputed from the received $T'$ at verification. An earlier draft of this
work claimed the opposite; Definition~\ref{def:system} is the corrected form,
and the correction matters because it is what makes
Proposition~\ref{thm:gap} a statement about \emph{where} the proved string is
delivered rather than about whether it is bound at all.

\subsection{Threat model}
\label{sec:threat}

The adversary is the \emph{agent operator}: the party that holds the policy, runs
the model, and produces the certificate. The verifier is a regulator, auditor, or
third party holding only public values. The policy author issues $\pi$; in this
section we treat it as honest, and \S\ref{sec:wp4-tension} makes that assumption
explicit and scopes it, because in policy-private mode its honesty is doing real
work and cannot be waved through. We state the goals, and more importantly the
non-goals, before the theorems.

\begin{assumption}[Adversary side information]
\label{as:Z}
The adversary knows the prompt, the model (possibly its weights), and the
decoding parameters. \emph{This is the assumption that makes the theorem
non-trivial}: under greedy decoding, $k \approx 0$.
\end{assumption}

\paragraph{Goals.}
\begin{description}
  \item[(G1)] \emph{Soundness}---a response $T$ violating $\pi$ cannot yield an
  accepted certificate.
  \item[(G2)] \emph{Committed privacy}---in private mode the public values
  contain no plaintext of $T$. This is explicitly \emph{not} the claim that $T$
  is unrecoverable: once the guessing entropy $k$ of Definition~\ref{def:k} falls
  below $\log q + \log(1/\varepsilon)$, a publicly recomputable binding and
  content hiding are mutually exclusive (Theorem~\ref{thm:t1}).
  \item[(G3)] \emph{Traceable binding}---policy version, circuit, and proof
  artefact are bound to one another and recomputed at verification.
  \item[(G4)] \emph{Selective disclosure}---redaction and evidence opening are
  themselves provable.
  \item[(G5)] \emph{Record integrity}---tampering with, or reordering, the audit
  ledger is detectable.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Non-goals.} We do not prove the model's inference, and we do not cover
training data or model cards. (G1) is modulo the soundness of the zkVM and of the
hash function; we use those primitives and do not re-derive them.

\paragraph{Where the policy-private case is developed.} The three-party model,
the issuing prerequisite (P-PP), and the table of what each verifier may and may
not conclude are specific to policy-private mode and are stated once, in
\S\ref{sec:wp4-tension}. The general adversary here is the same operator, viewed
without the policy-secrecy refinement.

\section{Binding of Policy Proofs}
\label{sec:binding}

A policy proof is meant to bind a response: a verifier who accepts a
certificate should conclude that \emph{this} string was checked against the
policy. This section asks what that binding is actually worth. We first isolate
the gap that survives in every design we surveyed (\S\ref{sec:gap}), then give
a quantitative bound on what a public value reveals, together with the
structural hypothesis that bound needs (\S\ref{sec:tradeoff}), and finally
state the same fact on the request side, where the governing resource is
freshness rather than entropy (\S\ref{sec:t3}).

\subsection{The gap}
\label{sec:gap}

\begin{proposition}[Divergent delivery]
\label{thm:gap}
Definition \ref{def:system} binds $T'$ \emph{as received by the verifying party}. If the
provider delivers a different string $T'' \ne T'$ to the end user, verification
still succeeds on $T'$. Neither hardware nor a proof can prevent this; only the
end user holding $n$ and recomputing $b$ can.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$b$ is a function of $(n,T)$ only; the delivery channel to the end user carries
no authenticated term. One line.
\end{proof}

Two remarks scope this proposition. First, it describes a \emph{residual} gap,
not the claim that policy proofs bind nothing: our system binds $T$ through the
verifier-issued nonce $n$, so a verifier receiving $T'$ and recomputing $b$ does
learn that $T'$ was the proved string; the gap is that the binding is anchored
to the \emph{verifying party's} copy. Second, we do not claim primacy for the
observation that a proof can fail to bind its artifact: Hollow-LLM
\cite{hollowllm} and zk-attach \cite{zkattach} name other gaps, both about
\emph{what} the proof system was asked to prove, whereas
Proposition~\ref{thm:gap} concerns \emph{where} the proved string is delivered.
We state the naive version of our own claim because it would be false against
our own system.

\subsection{A binding--hiding--verifiability framework}
\label{sec:tradeoff}

A certificate must publish \emph{something} tying it to the secret it certifies;
otherwise it binds nothing at all. We ask what that public value leaks, and we
ask the question uniformly for the response $T$, the policy $\pi$, and the
challenge on the request side. The answer depends on one measurable quantity,
the guessing entropy, and on one structural property: whether the binding can be
recomputed by anyone without a secret input.

\begin{definition}[Guessing entropy]
\label{def:k}
For a secret distribution $\mathcal{D}$ and adversary side information $Z$, let
\begin{multline*}
\mathsf{Guess}(X \mid Z) \;\triangleq\; \max_{\mathcal{A}} \Pr[\mathcal{A}(Z) = X]\\
\;=\; \mathbb{E}_z\!\left[\max_x \Pr[X = x \mid Z = z]\right],
\end{multline*}
\[
k_X \;\triangleq\; -\log_2 \mathsf{Guess}(X \mid Z),
\]
the maximum ranging over all time-unbounded adversaries.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[The definition is operational, and it is the average]
\label{rem:kmeas}
$k_X$ is \emph{measured}, not assumed: sampling one model on one prompt many
times estimates $\mathsf{Guess}(T \mid Z)$ directly (\S\ref{sec:eval-k}). The
definition must average over $z$ rather than take the pointwise worst case
$k_z = -\log \max_t \Pr[T = t \mid Z = z]$: the proof takes an expectation over
$z$, and $\mathbb{E}_z[2^{-k_z}] = 2^{-k_X}$ holds only for the average form.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Two strengths of public verifiability.} \emph{(PV-1)} asks that some
PPT verifier, holding public values and no secret state, check the proof.
\emph{(PV-2)} asks that there be a PPT algorithm $E$ with no secret input such
that $E(X)=\beta(X)$ for every $X$: anyone can recompute the binding offline and
compare it with the delivered artifact. The distinction is not pedantic. A keyed
binding $\beta_s(X)=H(s,X)$ can retain PV-1---a zero-knowledge proof still
verifies---while losing PV-2, because a verifier without $s$ cannot recompute
$\beta_s$. The trilemma below is a statement about PV-2. Our response binding
$b=H(\texttt{pop-bind-v1}\|n\|T)$ satisfies PV-2; a salted policy commitment
does not.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional trilemma]
\label{thm:t1}
\label{thm:trilemma}
Fix $\lambda,\varepsilon>0$ and $q\ge 1$. Let $X$ be a secret with guessing
entropy $k_X<\log q+\log(1/\varepsilon)$ whose binding is \emph{guess-tight}:
$\beta_\rho(X)=H(\mathsf{DOM}\|\rho\|X)$ for a random oracle $H$ and public
randomness $\rho$ independent of $X$ and $Z$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(1)] \emph{Public binding.} For any adversary that evaluates $\beta$ at
most $q$ times,
\begin{multline*}
\Pr\!\left[\mathcal{A}^\beta(Z,\beta(X))=X\right]\\
\le q\cdot\mathsf{Guess}(X\mid Z)+\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)
= q\cdot 2^{-k_X}+\mathrm{negl}(\lambda).
\end{multline*}
Hiding with advantage $\varepsilon$ is \emph{certified} by this bound when
$k_X\ge\log q+\log(1/\varepsilon)$; for a skewed distribution this is a
sufficient condition, not a matching attack. The integer $q$ counts evaluations
of $\beta$, not time: the adversary may be computationally unbounded.
\item[(2)] \emph{Keyed binding.} For $\beta_s(X)=H(s,X)$ with $s$ held only by a
designated party and absent from the public values,
$\Pr[\mathcal{A}(Z,b)=X]\le\mathsf{Guess}(X\mid Z)+\mathrm{negl}(\kappa)$.
The correct reading is \emph{not} ``hiding survives even when $k_X=0$'': if
$k_X=0$ then $Z$ already determines $X$, and no binding can help. What the keyed
form buys is hiding when $k_X<\log q+\log(1/\varepsilon)$, i.e.\ when the public
form would fall to enumeration. Its price is PV-2.
\item[(3)] \emph{Conditional trilemma.} Consider schemes whose binding is
publicly recomputable (PV-2), whose hiding is exact recovery, and whose binding
is guess-tight. Within the scheme class defined above, when
$k_X<\log q+\log(1/\varepsilon)$ such a scheme cannot satisfy binding, hiding
and PV-2 simultaneously: public recomputability gives (1), and avoiding (1)
requires a keyed binding, which forfeits PV-2.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
(1) Fix $z$ and decompose by the first query that hits $X$; conditioned on no
earlier hit, every answer already seen is uniform and independent of $X$, so
each query succeeds with probability at most $2^{-k_z}$; taking the expectation
over $z$ gives $q\cdot 2^{-k_X}$. The random-oracle idealisation and the SHA-256
instantiation are collected in $\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$. (2) Without an oracle
for $\beta_s$, the value $b$ is uniform and independent of $X$, so the best
strategy is the prior mode. (3) The three legs are excluded in turn as stated.
Section~S1 of the supplementary material gives the full argument.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[A classification, not an impossibility theorem]
\label{rem:trilemma-scope}
Proposition~\ref{thm:t1}(3) is a \emph{conditional classification}, not an
impossibility theorem: its hypotheses are PV-2, exact-recovery hiding, a
guess-tight binding, and a near-uniform candidate set. The escapes are real:
PV-1 with a keyed binding plus a zero-knowledge proof; trusted hardware; an
interactive or designated verifier; a high-entropy secret, where the condition
does not trigger; a relaxed, indistinguishability-based hiding goal; and
$k_X=0$, where hiding has already failed. For a skewed distribution the bound
certifies only that \emph{this upper bound cannot certify} $\varepsilon$-hiding;
it does not exhibit a matching attack.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The guess-tight hypothesis is necessary]
\label{rem:betanecessary}
Without it, part (1) is false rather than merely unproven: for
$\beta=\mathrm{id}$ or $\beta=\mathsf{encode}(T)$ the adversary outputs
$\beta(T)$ and wins with probability $1$, independently of $q$ and $k_X$. For
an arbitrary deterministic public $\beta$ the correct bound is over the
preimage class,
\[
\Pr\!\left[\mathcal{A}^\beta(Z,\beta(T))=T\right]\;\le\;
\mathbb{E}_y\!\left[\max_t \Pr[T=t \mid Z,\beta(T)=y]\right],
\]
which may equal $1$. Our two public values straddle the hypothesis---$b$ is
guess-tight, $\mathsf{encode}(T)$ is not---which makes the pair a statement
about \emph{our design} rather than a citation of a standard bound.
\end{remark}

\need{BLOCKER (theorem-level, no external resource): the bounds are random-oracle
heuristics. Replacing the random oracle with a standard-model assumption for
SHA-256 would require a different proof, which is not available; the paper
therefore claims these bounds only in the random-oracle model and records the
SHA-256 instantiation loss in $\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$.}

\begin{corollary}[Re-execution, not enumeration]
\label{cor:reexec}
Under Assumption~\ref{as:Z}, when decoding is greedy or low-temperature,
$k_T\approx 0$ and the attack of Proposition~\ref{thm:t1} is realised by
\emph{re-running the model}, at the cost of one inference---independent of $|T|$
and requiring \emph{no} evaluation of $\beta$. Enumeration over the string space
is \emph{not} the relevant attack: $2^{100}$ is as unenumerable as $2^{256}$.
\end{corollary}

\paragraph{Public versus secret challenge.} The bound $q\cdot2^{-k_T}$ is the
\emph{$n$-public} form: one evaluation of $\beta$ tests one candidate $T$. If
$n$ is secret---a designated verifier supplies it out of band---an evaluation
must guess both $n$ and $T$, giving $(1+q\cdot2^{-f})\cdot2^{-k_T}$ with
$f=|n|$. The price of the secret form is PV-2: a third party cannot recompute
$b$ offline. Table~\ref{tab:regimes} separates the two rows.

\begin{remark}[A design observation, not a separation]
\label{rem:designated}
Our verification interface is \emph{designated}: binding is checked against
$b=H(\mathsf{dom}\|n\|T)$, where $n$ is a verifier-issued nonce. It is tempting
to promote this into a separation (``public verification is impossible when $k$
is small''). \textbf{We claim no such theorem.} When $k\approx 0$, hiding fails
for \emph{every} scheme, designated or not, because the adversary re-runs the
model; when $k$ is large, a zero-knowledge proof of
``$\exists T: b=H(\mathsf{dom}\|n\|T)\wedge R(\pi,T)=1$'' is simultaneously
publicly verifiable and hiding. The dichotomy is governed by $k$ alone.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The leakage profile: one theorem, three regimes}
\label{sec:leak}

Proposition~\ref{thm:t1} assumed that the certificate depends on $X$ only
through its public binding. A real certificate also ships a proof artefact, and
the verifier has prior information. The following decomposition makes the three
contributions explicit.

\begin{theorem}[Leakage-profile decomposition]
\label{thm:leak}
Let $V=(L_{\mathrm{pub}},L_{\mathrm{proof}},L_{\mathrm{side}})$ be the verifier's
view, where $L_{\mathrm{pub}}$ is the certificate's public values,
$L_{\mathrm{proof}}$ is what the proof artefact reveals about the witness
$w=(\pi,T,\mathrm{trace})$, and $L_{\mathrm{side}}$ is the adversary's prior
information $Z$. For any $q\ge 1$ and any adversary that evaluates $\beta$ at
most $q$ times,
\begin{multline*}
\Pr\!\left[\mathcal{A}^\beta(V)=X\right]\\
\;\le\; q\cdot\mathsf{Guess}\!\left(X\mid L_{\mathrm{pub}},L_{\mathrm{proof}},L_{\mathrm{side}}\right)
+\mathrm{negl}(\lambda),
\end{multline*}
with the final output charged as one evaluation.
\end{theorem}

Again, $q$ counts evaluations of $\beta$, not time. The inequality itself is
close to the definition of guessing entropy when $q\ge1$: an adversary without
a $\beta$ oracle has $\Pr[\mathcal{A}(V)=X]\le\mathsf{Guess}(X\mid V)$. The content of Theorem~\ref{thm:leak} is the
\emph{decomposition} in Table~\ref{tab:regimes}, which turns one bound into
three claims about three proving regimes. In particular, a witness-hiding proof
sets $L_{\mathrm{proof}}=\bot$ and reduces the theorem to the public-value bound;
it does not touch $L_{\mathrm{side}}$.

\begin{proposition}[The lossy instance]
\label{prop:semantic}
For the semantic rule class, the public values contain

preimage-class bound: the binding force is exactly
$\mathsf{Guess}(T\mid Z,\mathsf{encode}(T))$. The adversary's search space is the
preimage of the published vector, not the string space; the relevant asymmetry
is a \emph{lossy public encoding}, not ``low entropy''.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{The policy side: D-privacy and a salted commitment}
\label{sec:policy-side}

The same accounting applies to the policy. The policy text is a witness; only
$\mathsf{policy\_hash}=H(\mathsf{canonical}(\pi))$ is public. But that hash is a
public, offline-recomputable function of $\pi$---the same mathematical object as
$b$---so ``the policy is a witness'' closes the \emph{text} channel and not the
\emph{hash} channel. Before stating the privacy goal we fix a defect in the
naive formulation.

\paragraph{The artifact must be fixed.} If the two worlds may use different
artifacts $T_0\neq T_1$, the verifier distinguishes them by computing $B(n,T_0)$
and $B(n,T_1)$ once. That is response-side leakage, not policy leakage.
Accordingly the game fixes one artifact $T$ and one challenge $n$, or
equivalently requires the two artifacts to have the same public $T$-view; only
$\pi_0$ and $\pi_1$ differ.

\begin{definition}[D-privacy]
\label{def:dpriv}
Let $D:\Pi\to\mathbb{D}$ be the disclosure function. A scheme is
$D$-private if for every PPT adversary, every $(\pi_0,\pi_1,T,n)$ with
$D(\pi_0)=D(\pi_1)$ and $R(\pi_0,T)=R(\pi_1,T)$, the views
$\mathsf{View}_b=(L_{\mathrm{pub}}(\pi_b,T,n),L_{\mathrm{proof}}(\pi_b,T,n),
L_{\mathrm{side}})$ for $b\in\{0,1\}$ are computationally indistinguishable.
Formally, the challenger samples $b\leftarrow\{0,1\}$ and returns
$\mathsf{View}_b$; the adversary outputs $b'$, and the advantage is
$\mathsf{Adv}_{CS,D}=|2\Pr[b'=b]-1|$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[D-privacy from three conditions]
\label{thm:dpriv}
The scheme is $D$-private if (C1) there is a PPT simulator $S_{\mathrm{rest}}$
such that, for every admissible $(\pi,T,n)$, the public values other than the
commitment satisfy
$(h_\pi,S_{\mathrm{rest}}(pp,h_\pi,D(\pi),\mathrm{pub}_T,n))\approx_c
L_{\mathrm{pub}}(\pi,T,n)$; (C2) the proof system is witness-hiding, i.e.\
$L_{\mathrm{proof}}$ has a simulator that takes only the public statement;
and (C3) the policy commitment is hiding for all $D$-equal policies,
$(pp,h_{\pi_0})\approx_c(pp,h_{\pi_1})$. The advantage is
$O(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pub}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{wh}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{com}})$,
by a five-hybrid argument.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Hybrids replace the proof artefact by its witness-hiding simulator (C2), the
remaining public values by $S_{\mathrm{rest}}$ (C1), and the commitment by one
for a $D$-equal policy (C3); the triangle inequality over six hybrids gives the
stated advantage. Section~S1 of the supplementary material gives the full
argument.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The salted construction.} To satisfy (C3), the authority samples a
$128$-bit nonce $s$ per policy package and commits to the canonical bytes with
$s$ as an explicit field,
\[
h_\pi=\mathsf{SHA256}\bigl(\mathsf{canonical}(\pi,s)\bigr),\qquad
s\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{128},
\]
where $\mathsf{canonical}$ is the sorted, compact JSON encoding of the compiled
constraints with \texttt{policy\_nonce} set to $s$. The nonce enters the
\texttt{ConstraintSpec} bytes and therefore the public \texttt{policy\_hash}; it
does \emph{not} enter the certificate's \texttt{policy.id}/\texttt{version}
fields, so the verifier does not see it. The authority registers $h_\pi$ in the
issuing record and opens $(\pi,s)$ during an audit, where the verifier recomputes
$h_\pi$. Without $s$, an enumeration over candidate policies must also guess the
salt: the work is multiplied by $2^{128}$, and the qualitative statement is all
we claim---we do not assert an exact exponent of the form $2^{-(\lambda+128)}$.%
\footnote{With $q_s$ salt guesses per candidate, recovery is at most
$q_s/2^{128}+|\Pi_c|q_s/2^{\lambda}$; exhausting the salt space gives an
aggregate false-positive term $|\Pi_c|/2^{\lambda-128}$. The salt does not
lengthen the $\lambda$-bit hash output, so $|\Pi_c|/2^{\lambda+128}$ is not the
right exponent.}

\paragraph{What the salt does not close.} Theorem~\ref{thm:dpriv} is
conditional, and two conditions fail in the current system. (C1) fails when the
public values contain \texttt{PrivateViolation\{rule,kind\}} or a non-empty
\texttt{delegated} (Table~\ref{tab:wp4}, rows 4 and 6): both depend on the
undisclosed structure of $\pi$, so a disclosure level $D$ that does not cover
them is not a $D$-privacy level at all.
(C2) fails at the default \texttt{core} tier: the trace carries
\texttt{spec\_canonical}, which contains both $\pi$ and the salt, so a
witness-bearing artifact recovers $s$ and then $\pi$ by enumeration. The salt
closes the public-value channel only; it is not ``policy privacy solved,'' and
\S\ref{sec:eval} measures the disclosure-level channel that remains open.

\subsection{Quantitative request binding}
\label{sec:t3}

\begin{theorem}[Delivery binding]
\label{thm:t3}
Fix a policy $\pi$. A delivery adversary $\mathcal{B}$ may, \emph{before}
seeing the receiver's challenge, precompute a set $S$ of accepted transcripts,
where each transcript covers at most $c$ challenges. The receiver then
contributes $f$ fresh, uniformly random bits $r$ that enter the proven
statement. Under the certificate's soundness (D1) and challenge binding
(D2/B-bind), the divergence advantage satisfies
\[
\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathrm{del}}(\mathcal{B}) \;\le\; |S| \cdot c \cdot 2^{-f}
\;+\; \mathrm{negl}(\lambda).
\]
The replay-only probability---acceptance without requiring divergence---is at
most $|S|\cdot c\cdot 2^{-f}$. If the receiver contributes no fresh randomness
($f = 0$; e.g.\ an empty nonce), a precomputed transcript is accepted with
probability $1$ and \emph{does not decay in any parameter of the system};
divergence in that case is bounded only by the binding failure
$\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
For a certificate in $S$ to be accepted on $r$, $r$ must fall in that
certificate's challenge set of size at most $c$; a union bound over $S$ gives
$|S|\cdot c\cdot 2^{-f}$. A certificate minted after $r$ is seen can diverge
only by exhibiting two preimages of $b$ under $B(r,\cdot)$, a challenge-binding
failure costing $\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$; with $f=0$ every element of $S$ is a
permanent replay, so the probability is $1$. Section~S1 gives the full proof.
\end{proof}

Call the acceptance-only game $G_{\mathrm{rep}}$ and the divergent-delivery
game $G_{\mathrm{del}}$. The bound $|S|\cdot c\cdot2^{-f}$ is the
$G_{\mathrm{rep}}$ acceptance bound; under D2, divergence in $G_{\mathrm{del}}$
is only $\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$. The $f=0$ probability $1$ is therefore a
statement about $G_{\mathrm{rep}}$ (or about a non-binding $B$), not about
$G_{\mathrm{del}}$ under D2.

Theorem~\ref{thm:t3} completes the spine. It is the request-side instance of the
same accounting that Proposition~\ref{thm:t1} and Theorem~\ref{thm:dpriv} apply
to the response and the policy: a proof binds only as much as the committed
quantity the adversary cannot select in advance has bits for. On the response
side that quantity is $T$ and the resource is entropy $k_T$; on the policy side
it is $\pi$ and the resource is $k_\pi$ or the $128$-bit salt; on the request
side it is freshness $f$. None of the three can be bought with more computation.
The $c$ factor matters: one certificate may cover several challenges, so a
precomputed set of size $|S|$ purchases $|S|\cdot c$ attempts, not $|S|$.

\begin{remark}[A taxonomy, not a trichotomy]
\label{rem:taxonomy}
Every policy-proof deployment we surveyed closes the divergent-delivery gap in
one of three ways: (a) root trust in hardware; (b) have the \emph{end user}
contribute a secret to the proven statement; or (c) gate delivery on successful
verification. \textbf{This is a classification, not an exhaustiveness theorem}:
we neither prove that a fourth family cannot exist nor that the three are
disjoint. zk-attach \cite{zkattach} walks (a); NiyamAI and ZKAP walk (c); PoP
walks (b).
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Posture, stated plainly.} Our system carries
\texttt{response\_binding}, but $n$ is issued by the \emph{verifier}. Moving it
to the \emph{end user} makes $f > 0$ the receiver's contribution and closes the
divergent-delivery branch of Proposition~\ref{thm:gap}. The certificate layer
supports an end-user issuer (\texttt{end\_user\_binding}, domain
\texttt{pop-bind-enduser-v1}), but the circuit still computes the verifier-domain
binding, so the end-to-end form needs a circuit change and a new vkey; until then
this is a certificate-layer amendment, not a drop-in issuer swap. What
Theorem~\ref{thm:t3} buys is the \emph{price}: with $f = 0$ there is no closure
at any cost.

\section{Policy-Private Compliance}
\label{sec:private-policy}

For a policy whose rules are deterministic and a response that is public
anyway, a signed hash chain over the response suffices: the signature already
binds the bytes, and anyone can re-evaluate the rules. The one scenario a
signature cannot cover is a policy that must itself stay secret---where the
operator has to certify compliance to a third party without handing over the
rules it is being judged against. That is the mode this section is about, and we
lead with what the mode \emph{cannot} buy, because that determines whether it
means anything at all.

\subsection{Two channels, not one}
\label{sec:wp4-finding}

\texttt{spec\_canonical} is already a \emph{guest input}---a witness---rather
than a public value; only its hash,
\[
\mathsf{policy\_hash} \;=\; \mathsf{SHA256}(\texttt{spec\_canonical}),
\]
enters the public values (\texttt{circuits/\allowbreak types/\allowbreak
src/\allowbreak lib.rs}:1--16, 415--416, 918, 1003--1004). It is tempting to
conclude that the policy text is already hidden. It is not: ``do not reveal the
policy text'' closes the \emph{text} channel, while
$\mathsf{policy\_hash}$ remains a public, offline-recomputable function of the
policy---the same object as $b$, and therefore the same enumeration target. The
space is small (eight rule classes, bounded parameters, three to eight rules), so
a candidate policy can be compiled and matched against the published hash.

Two further channels survive: the proof artefact at the default \texttt{core}
tier, and public-value fields derived from the undisclosed structure of $\pi$.
Table~\ref{tab:wp4} states the channels and what closes each; the salted
commitment of \S\ref{sec:policy-side} closes only the first.

\subsection{Disclosure levels and the inference they permit}
\label{sec:eval-policy}

If the policy cannot be hidden behind a hash alone, the practical question is
how much of it to disclose. We implement the levels \emph{in the circuit}: the
level is a private input, the guest coarsens each violation before commitment,
and the public value carries a \texttt{pop-pv-v2} schema and the committed
level. L0 emits the raw rule and kind; L1 one bucket per category with its
count and the total; L2 only one opaque commitment per violation (whose list
length still leaks the count). Evidence plaintext is absent at every level. On
the cloud-built circuit (vkey \texttt{0x000cfdfc\ldots}), core proofs for
L0/L1/L2 all verify; the L1 public value contains only
\texttt{category="content"}, \texttt{count=1}, the total and a
commitment---no rule name, kind or evidence. The verifier checks the schema,
level, shape and equality with the certificate payload; it does not re-project.

This is an MVP, not a closed channel. Delegated category buckets are not
implemented, so L1/L2 fail closed for policies with \texttt{semantic\_bound};
the host mirror, session aggregation and policy-corpus benchmark still use v1;
old v1 proofs are rejected; and all proofs are \texttt{core}, whose artefact
still exposes the witness, so we do not claim hiding from a party that holds
the proof. Table~\ref{tab:wp4} records these open channels.

We measured what each level permits with an inference attack over 20 policy
variants, using 40 training and 15 test responses per variant and a standard
multinomial naive Bayes classifier (seed 20261101). The responses are
\emph{template-generated synthetic strings}---11 templates per variant
(\texttt{bench\_policy\_infer.py::\_responses})---not real LLM outputs, so the
curve estimates leakage from the projection, not deployment risk. Features are
drawn only from the certificate public values of each level; the random-guess
baseline over 20 classes is $0.050$. Table~\ref{tab:policy-infer} reports the
result.

interpretable, which is a property of the payload; and the numbers estimate
curve shape on fixed corpora, not deployment-specific risk.

The structural lesson is the one Table~\ref{tab:wp4} states: the salt closes the
hash channel, the disclosure level governs the certificate channel, and neither
closes the proof-artefact channel. ``Policy privacy solved'' would require all
three, and the third is still blocked on a witness-hiding tier
(\S\ref{sec:eval-proofs}).

\subsection{The tension worth writing up: policy privacy vs.\ accountability}
\label{sec:wp4-tension}

\paragraph{The prerequisite, stated as a prerequisite.} Let $h_\pi$ be the
policy commitment. On its own $h_\pi$ is \emph{inert}. A prover can always
emit a self-consistent certificate for any policy $\pi'$ it prefers, simply by
placing $h_{\pi'}$ in that certificate. \emph{Secrecy of $\pi$ is what creates
the gap}, and no cryptographic mechanism can close it: closing it would require
showing $\pi$ to the verifier, which is precisely what the mode exists to avoid.
What closes it is an \emph{issuing act}, so we state it as an explicit
prerequisite of the mode.

\begin{description}
  \item[(P-PP)] An \emph{issuing authority} signs $\pi$, registers $h_\pi$ in a
  record readable by third parties, and holds the salt. A policy is
  \emph{accountable} iff its \texttt{policy\_hash} can be traced to such a
  record.
\end{description}

Without (P-PP) the mode is not merely weaker---the verifier's conclusion is
empty. This is the same assumption as the ``honest policy author'' of
\texttt{docs/security-model.md}~\S0.2; what is new is scoping it to the
verification interface, which we are not aware of prior work doing, and which a
referee would otherwise use to dismiss the whole mode in one sentence.

\paragraph{Three parties.} ``Three'' means \emph{authority / operator / third
party}; an auditor is a named instance of the third party, not a fourth trust
anchor. The authority holds $\pi$ and the issuing record; the operator is the
adversary; the third party holds only public values and must not learn $\pi$.
Table~S8 of the supplementary material states the full model and its two
boundaries: secrecy comes from a data-flow fact, not from zero-knowledge, and
auditor accountability is voluntary.

\paragraph{What each verifier may conclude.} Table~S9 of the supplementary
material is the contribution of this subsection: the cryptography is downstream
of it, and the table survives even if the cryptography only partially lands. A
third party $V$ may conclude only that (i) the commitment binds $T$, (ii)
\emph{some} policy with commitment $h_\pi$ passed, and (iii) $h_\pi$ appears in
the issuing record; it may \emph{not} conclude the content of $\pi$, how many
rules it has, or ``the operator enforces the regulation \emph{I} care about''.
The end user additionally learns that the proved $T$ is the string it received,
provided $f>0$. Conflating ``some issued policy was satisfied'' with
``compliance'' is the sentence a referee would use to reject the mode, and
preventing that sentence is what the table is for. The end-user binding is
currently a certificate-layer field; the end-to-end form needs a circuit change
and a new vkey (\S\ref{sec:t3}).

\paragraph{Prior art.} \emph{Policy hiding is not new}, and we claim no primacy.
Hidden credentials \cite{hiddencred}, secret handshakes \cite{handshake},
privacy-preserving XACML access control \cite{xacmlpriv}, and predicate
encryption \cite{sw05,ksw08} are one family; policy-private mode is an instance.
What is new is narrow: (a) the \emph{application} to LLM output compliance with
selective disclosure of violation type and location, and (b) the accountability
tension above.

\need{BLOCKER (author decision): the instantiation of (P-PP)---an on-chain
registry of issued policy commitments versus a signed manifest---is not fixed;
both are compatible with the text. The five prior-art citations are verified in
\texttt{docs/citation-audit.md}.}

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Cycle count: length versus rule count}
\label{sec:eval-cycles}

Response length was pushed from 200 to 100{,}000 characters and the rule count
from 1 to 6, giving 40 measurement points (\texttt{bench/bench\_cycles.py}; the
full matrix is Table~S1 and \texttt{bench/results/cycles.md}). The response text
comes from an evaluation artefact the repository's own demo produced (372
characters); longer points are tiled from it. What is measured is therefore the
\emph{curve} of scanning cost, not ``a real 100{,}000-character document.'' Every
point was first confirmed to match no rule: a matching rule short-circuits, and
the cost of a short-circuit says nothing.

Three findings. \emph{(1) With the rule set fixed, cycle count is exactly linear
in length}: five rule sets, six length points each, $R^2 \approx 1.0000$
throughout. \emph{(2) Scanning cost is dominated by the NFA, not the rule
count}: one keyword rule costs about $+31.6$ cycles per character at the margin;
one NFA regular expression costs about $+3{,}634$---roughly $115\times$ more.
Equivalently, a rule set containing a regular expression has a per-character
slope ($4{,}289.8$) $58\times$ that of string rules only ($73.9$).
\emph{(3) Unit prices are not additive.} Fitting the slopes as
$(\text{keyword},\text{pattern})$ unit prices gives $R^2 = 0.9169$ and a
\emph{negative} keyword unit price ($-477.2$ cycles per character). An ordering
probe (same rules and text, $L=10^5$) costs $541{,}342{,}069$ cycles in declared
order against $570{,}736{,}989$ in reverse, $+5.43\%$, while \texttt{passed} is
identical: the semantics do not depend on order, the cost does. A marginal probe
gives $+31.6$ for one more keyword rule alone, $+585.6$ with a regular
expression already present, and $-153.7$ if inserted before it. The mechanism is
zkVM allocator state; we therefore quote per-rule-set slopes rather than any
``unit price $\times$ count'' expression. The model
$\text{cycles} \approx a\cdot|T|\cdot\text{rules} + b$ is refuted by the data
($R^2 = 0.872$, negative intercept), and we report that too: \emph{a model
refuted by its own data is itself an evaluation result.}

\subsection{Matcher ablation: quadratic degradation on a pathological input}
\label{sec:eval-ablation}

Pattern \texttt{a+b} against input \texttt{a}$^{\times n}$ containing no
\texttt{b}: the match is necessarily absent and both implementations must scan
to the end. The naive implementation reinvents and restarts at every start
position, giving $\Theta(n^2)$; the Pike VM decides in a single linear pass
(\texttt{bench/bench\_ablation.py}). At $n=100,200,400$ the naive path consumes
$12{,}382{,}720$, $48{,}638{,}946$ and $193{,}207{,}130$ cycles against Pike's
$643{,}894$, $1{,}145{,}209$ and $2{,}143{,}617$---ratios of $19.2\times$,
$42.5\times$ and $90.1\times$ (Table~S2 and
\texttt{bench/results/ablation.md}). Per-character, doubling $n$ leaves Pike
essentially unchanged ($-11.1\%$, $-6.4\%$) while the naive path nearly doubles
($+96.4\%$, $+98.6\%$). \emph{This is an attack surface}: response length is
chosen externally, so an adversary who writes a long reply drives a naive
implementation's cost quadratically. The comparison is meaningful only because
both paths decide identically, which \texttt{tests/test\_ablation.py} pins down.

\paragraph{Proof-path cost.} Re-running the construction through the compliance
driver reproduces the ratios ($643{,}906$ against $12{,}382{,}732$ cycles at
$n=100$, rising to $90.1\times$ at $n=400$; Table~S3) and prices the proving
path. Pike at $n=100$ succeeds at $122.2$\,s and $11{,}695$\,MiB; naive at
$n=25$ at $153.2$\,s and $12{,}137$\,MiB; at $n=50$ it is
\textbf{OOM/SIGKILL} after $226.5$\,s at $13{,}772$\,MiB peak. We report the OOM
as a result rather than a discarded run: it is where the adversarial quadratic
term exhausts a $14.6$\,GiB machine, and why the $n\ge100$ naive points and all
$n=400$ proving points are left to a cloud machine. These runs predate the NFA
fix of \S\ref{sec:disc-dos} and carry that binary's digest; the fix changes the
guest and vkey, so the figures are not mixed with any post-fix run.

\subsection{The non-ZK baseline: what a signature cannot do}
\label{sec:eval-baseline}

A signed Merkle log is the obvious alternative to a proof, and on cost it is not
close: everything is milliseconds. We measured it so the comparison is not
rhetorical. Policy package \texttt{eu\_ai\_act\_v1.json}, 103-character response,
one challenge nonce, a 1024-leaf log; each timing is the median of $n = 500$
repetitions (\texttt{bench/results/baseline.md}): sign certificate $0.132$\,ms,
Merkle append $2.131$\,ms, public verification $0.322$\,ms, re-run verification
$0.772$\,ms; certificate $648$\,B, inclusion proof $761$\,B. Two caveats: the
append is a \emph{static tree recomputation} in \texttt{proofs/session.py},
$\Theta(n)$, not the amortised $\Theta(\log n)$ of an RFC-6962 incremental log;
and the measurements come from a shared machine with $\pm 20\%$ drift. This arm
is a \emph{feasibility} baseline---the sizes are prototype sizes, not evidence
that a public transparency log is deployed.

The asymmetry is the point. The verification chain is \emph{verify signature}
$\to$ \emph{verify log inclusion} $\to$ \emph{re-run $J(\pi,T)$}. The first two
need only public data; the third needs the plaintext of both $\pi$ and $T$. A
signature binds \emph{who said it} to the bytes, never \emph{whether it is
true}. We measured the consequence: \textbf{the same key signs a certificate
whose declared content is false, and the public path passes green}, because what
it sees is a valid signature and a valid inclusion proof. Only re-executing
$J(\pi,T)$ recovers the truth. One honest row: on \emph{unforgeability} the
baseline is \emph{not weaker} than the proof path, both being Ed25519 and vkey
binding, and we do not score a point for ZK there.

\subsection{Composition: four negative controls}
\label{sec:eval-compose}

Theorem~\ref{thm:leak} prices each half; it does not say that conjoining two
valid halves yields a valid composite. We built four cells against the composite
verifier's binding layer (Table~S4 and
\texttt{bench/results/composition\_controls.md}). Only cell~(a) satisfies the
strong form, and only because $x_U=\mathsf{derive}(T_U)$; the implementation
claims only the weak form of Proposition~\ref{thm:composition} and
Remark~\ref{rem:e1}. Cell~(b) uses different nonces and is rejected by the
four-way binding check. Cell~(c) is the one that matters: the verifier binds the
inference half to $\mathsf{derive}(T_U)$, not to the input $x_U$ the user
submitted, so it accepts a certificate proving
$\exists x: x=\mathsf{derive}(T_U)\wedge T_U=M(x)$ while the strong form
$x=x_U$ remains unproved. This is proof debt E-1, not a test artefact: it is why
composition alone does not close the gap. Cell~(d) with non-empty
\texttt{delegated} is accepted as a certificate but fails the policy outcome,
the intended fail-closed behaviour.

\subsection{Gateway signing: measured cost, blocked circuit}
\label{sec:eval-gateway}

Off-circuit Ed25519 verification costs $0.094$, $0.844$ and $10.165$\,ms per
receipt batch at $1$, $10$ and $100$ receipts, with a per-receipt marginal near
$0.08$--$0.10$\,ms ($n=300$ per row; Table~S5 and
\texttt{bench/results/gateway\_sig.md}); the session-end \texttt{ToolSeal} is
$0.074$\,ms to sign, $0.184$\,ms to verify and $345$\,B. A concatenated 2-of-3
threshold prototype signs in $0.173$\,ms, merges in $0.001$\,ms, verifies in
$0.508$\,ms and produces a $981$\,B certificate ($759$\,B when only the two
required signatures are carried). Threshold semantics are machine-checked: any
two distinct signatures pass, one is rejected, and two signatures under the same
key id do not count as two.

The in-circuit row is the honest blank. Verifying Ed25519 inside the zkVM is not
in the circuit---\texttt{circuits/} deliberately keeps the signature field out
of the circuit view---and building a guest to measure it exceeds this
experiment's file ownership, so cycles, proving time and peak memory are
\emph{null}, not zero. We do not infer a circuit cost from the off-circuit cost,
and the trust root still contains the gateway key. The threshold prototype buys
a weaker single point of trust, not cheaper verification: one compromised key is
no longer enough, at roughly three times the signing work and certificate size,
with a new obligation to argue that the gateways agree on the chain tail.
Putting Ed25519 inside the guest is a design option, not a measured one; SP1
6.7.0 has no Ed25519 precompile, and the guest change would rotate the policy
vkey and add public values, invalidating existing certificates. An authenticated
gateway roster, HSM key custody and a trusted time source remain open.

\subsection{Cross-system baselines: 49 cells, 46 populated}
\label{sec:eval-baselines}

Table~S6 places PoP against six alternatives across seven dimensions: 49 cells,
of which 46 are populated (measured or cited) and 3 are unavailable. Every
populated cell is tagged $[m]$ (measured here) or $[c]$ (cited). The three
measured arms are PoP, the signed-Merkle log and a simulated trusted server;
zkAgent, NiyamAI, Zaguk and PCO are cited. Zaguk and PCO did not yield
comparable performance numbers in our environment---the former's proceedings PDF
was unreachable and the latter reports no performance table---so those cells are
em dashes, not estimates. The dimensions impose different proof obligations; the
table states \emph{what each system obliges the verifier to trust}, not a single
ranking. The asymmetry is the one the signature baseline showed: the cheap arms
require re-running the judgment on plaintext, while the private arms move that
work into a proof or a trusted party. PoP is on the proof side, with a measured
cost four to five orders of magnitude above the signature arm and a trust root
that, unlike the TEE arm, contains no hardware.

\subsection{Issuance: detection, not prevention}
\label{sec:eval-issuance}

A provider that never issues a certificate is indistinguishable, from the
verifier's side, from a provider with nothing to certify; no cryptographic layer
forces issuance. What a transparency log changes is the \emph{detection} rate,
and only under an external obligation to append before delivery. In a simulation
with $N=2000$ deliveries, $m=100$ withheld violations and sampling rate
$p=0.05$, an audit that samples the \emph{delivered} artifacts detects $0.9948$
of the withheld set in exact arithmetic ($0.9940$ by Monte Carlo over 500
draws), while an audit that samples the \emph{log} detects $0.0000$, because the
withheld deliveries have no log entry at all: the sampling frame is wrong, not
unlucky (Table~S7 and \texttt{bench/results/issuance.md}).

Two boundaries travel with these numbers: the result quantifies
\emph{detection}, never \emph{prevention} (penalties and incentives are outside
the model); and sufficiency assumes an append-before-delivery obligation, an
append-only publicly readable log, inclusion proofs, and an external mechanism
against split views. The prototype implements append-only semantics, inclusion
proofs, optional tree-head signatures and checkpoints, but not consistency
proofs or gossip, so split-view resistance is an external assumption
(\S\ref{sec:disc-coverage}).
\subsection{End-to-end and auditing}
\label{sec:eval-e2e}

The end-to-end demo produces 14 certificates (streaming chain with early stop,
MCP argument and response checks, one zk proof) and anchors them; a third party
verifies with public artefacts alone---ledger chain, signature,
\texttt{policy\_hash}, anchoring, stream chains and zk proof all pass. In the
on-chain variant (local Anvil, \texttt{Anchor.sol}, 14 digests) the verifier
reads back each \texttt{anchoredAt} and cross-checks the ledger, 14/14; an
unregistered digest reads back as zero, so the check is not vacuous.
Cross-validation submits 19 vectors to the Python reference and both SP1 paths,
comparing \texttt{passed} and the violated rule set; a full re-run with real
\texttt{core} proofs gave 19/19. A DeepSeek API run made \textbf{8 real chat
calls and issued 32 certificates} across two sessions (13 and 19);
\texttt{verify\_session.py} returned \texttt{RESULT: PASS} on all 11 checks using
only the session file, ledger and signer public keys. Mean time-to-first-token
was $0.167$\,s, and host-side policy check plus signing averaged $10.8$\,ms per
turn. Streaming early stop triggered after a 24-character decision buffer with
22 characters delivered; because \texttt{sk-[A-Za-z0-9]\{16,\}} needs the
\texttt{sk-} prefix plus 16 characters, it prevents the \emph{complete} key and
subsequent tokens, not a short prefix. No SP1 proof was attached; every
certificate is \texttt{proof\_mode=unproven}, and PASS means the received bytes
were bound and re-evaluable, not that compliance was cryptographically proved.

\subsection{Measuring \texorpdfstring{$k$}{k}}
\label{sec:eval-k}

We measured $\mathsf{Guess}(T\mid Z)$ on the 50 XSTest prompts used by the
end-to-end runs. Exact-string mode counting cannot resolve entropy above the
sample count: $p_{\max}\ge1/N$, hence $k\le\log_2 N$. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with
$N=50$ produced 50 distinct responses in all 50 groups, so $k=5.644=\log_2 50$
is the \emph{estimator's resolution cap}, not a lower bound on $k$; Mistral-7B
and Qwen at $N=20$ likewise hit $\log_2 20=4.322$. The one informative
count-based point is DeepSeek-chat: 4 of 50 groups repeated a short refusal
template verbatim ($p_{\max}=0.10$, $k=3.32$). Larger $N$ did not resolve
Qwen's entropy.

A teacher-forced log-probability estimator removes the $N$ cap. It scores the
20 sampled responses per prompt under the sampling distribution at temperature
$0.7$ and sets $p_{\max}^{\rm est}=\max_i\Pr(T_i\mid Z)$. Because a maximum
over sampled sequences cannot exceed the true maximum,
$p_{\max}^{\rm est}\le p_{\max}$ and $k^{\rm est}\ge k$: this is an
\emph{upper bound}, so it can refute but never certify $k\ge40$. Qwen's median
$k^{\rm est}$ is $79.7$\,bit (range $12.1$--$210.8$) and Mistral's is
$63.2$\,bit ($12.6$--$111.6$); all 50 groups per model have
$p_{\max}^{\rm est}<1/N$ (Qwen's median is $22.7$ orders of magnitude below).
Yet $16/50$ Qwen and $11/50$ Mistral groups have $k^{\rm est}<40$, so their
true $k$ is provably below the threshold; for the remaining groups $k$ stays
unknown. At temperature $0$, all 50 groups have $k=0$, and one re-execution
recovers $T$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:reexec}). The 40-bit condition is therefore
not a universal property of these models: it fails on a measurable subset at
temperature $0.7$ and fails entirely under greedy decoding. Deployment must
constrain decoding and the query budget (duplicate/replay rejection), not
assume entropy.

Chat-template, refusal-proxy and keyword-false-positive details are in the
supplementary material.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We position PoP against five lineages; Table~S10 records each slot and what we
need not claim. \emph{Policy-to-circuit}: zkGen \cite{zkgen}, Circuitree,
Prezta. \emph{Agent/LLM compliance}: NiyamAI, Zaguk, PCO \cite{pco}, WITNESS.
\emph{Model computation}: DeepProve, zkGPT, zkAgent \cite{zkagent}.
\emph{Attacks and negative results}: Hollow-LLM \cite{hollowllm}, zk-attach
\cite{zkattach}. \emph{Non-ZK baselines}: \texttt{@brivora/verify}, VAOL.

The lineage this paper is in dialogue with is the fourth. Hollow-LLM's
\emph{effort gap} and zk-attach's \emph{source-integrity gap} both concern
\emph{what} the proof system was asked to prove.
Proposition~\ref{thm:gap} concerns a third gap on a different axis, \emph{where}
the proved string is delivered; in the systems we surveyed we are not aware of
prior work that quantifies the binding/hiding tradeoff in the terms of
\S\ref{sec:binding}, which is why we state it as a bound with a stated
hypothesis rather than cite it.

\paragraph{zkAgent: orthogonal, not competing.} zkAgent \cite{zkagent} is the
closest contemporaneous system, and the comparison is easy to get wrong in both
directions. It proves that a provider executed the declared model and tools; PoP
proves that a response satisfies a public policy. Neither implies the other, and
their conjunction covers ``inference was executed \emph{and} the policy held''.
Table~S11 places the reported figures side by side: zkAgent reports $194.4$\,s
proving (Shout, $T{=}512$), $96$\,MiB (Shout) / $3.1$\,MiB (LogUp) proofs, and
$0.42$\,s verification on 32 cores / 512\,GB; PoP measures 40.5--100.3\,s
(\texttt{core}) and 279--344\,s (\texttt{gr\-oth16}), 2.79\,MB / 1{,}874\,B, and
$\approx$0.090\,s verification on 16 cores / 90\,GiB. We do not claim PoP is
faster: \emph{for the compliance obligation}, it completes in a proving time of
the same order, with a smaller memory footprint and a comparable proof size,
without touching the model, and it adds committed content privacy. Hardware is
unmatched and PoP's ceiling is memory-bound, so its feasible region is a
non-rectangular set of measured points, not a curve
(\texttt{bench/comparison\_zkagent.md}).

The one influence we should name plainly, rather than leave to resemblance:
zk-regex's ``match offline, verify the path in circuit'' pattern is the shape of
our NFA contract, and verifiable document redaction \cite{vdr} inspired the bit
selector behind \texttt{mask\_covered}. The borrowing stops at the circuit
technique and does not carry the security argument across: VDR assumes a
\emph{signed original document} exists, whereas in PoP's private mode both the
original and the redacted version are produced by the operator, so there is no
trusted original to appeal to. We use the mechanism, not the proof.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

Everything above was kept deliberately narrow; this section says how narrow.

\subsection{The caliber of the privacy claims}
\label{sec:disc-privacy}
\label{sec:disc-policy}

\textbf{``Content privacy'' means \emph{no plaintext of $T$ in the public
values}, not \emph{content is unrecoverable}.} Two independent reasons force
this reading---one at the proof-system level, one at the design level.

\emph{Proof-system level.} The default proving tier is SP1's \texttt{core}, and
SP1's published security model states that individual STARK proofs do not
currently satisfy zero-knowledge; the source agrees, with no blinding in the
proof stack and the shard proof carrying the trace Merkle root and trace
openings in the clear.%
\footnote{\url{https://docs.succinct.xyz/docs/sp1/security/security-model\#groth16-plonk-and-the-zero-knowledgeness-of-sp1}, accessed 2026-11-01.}
Soundness (G1) therefore holds while the proof artefact does not hide the
witness. The \texttt{gr\-oth16} and \texttt{plonk} tiers wrap the inner STARK
proof as a private witness---the only mode in which hiding is even possible---but
that is a wrapper-level claim, it is not post-quantum, it needs $\ge 16$\,GB,
and we measured it in \S\ref{sec:eval-proofs}.

\emph{Design level, and the more fundamental one.} The public values
\texttt{response\_binding} and \texttt{response\_commitment} are both
\emph{public, offline-recomputable functions of $T$}. This is not an oversight:
\texttt{response\_binding} \emph{must} be publicly recomputable for the
swap-response defence, but the same property is a guess-and-verify oracle for
$T$. On a 1{,}451-character chat response, neither the response nor any line
appeared in delivered artefacts at either tier, while the public values
contained the violation rule name \texttt{no\_bad\_topics}. That is a
policy-field leak (Table~\ref{tab:wp4} row~4), not a response leak;
\texttt{core} reports \texttt{hiding=none}, \texttt{gr\-oth16}
\texttt{wrapper-only}; a zero canary count is a probe negative, not a
zero-knowledge proof.

The attack is \emph{not} enumeration: ``a one-line response carries only
$10^2$--$10^3$ bits of entropy'' does not make enumeration feasible---$2^{100}$
is as unenumerable as $2^{256}$. It is \emph{re-execution}
(Corollary~\ref{cor:reexec}): the side information (Assumption~\ref{as:Z}) is
(prompt, model, decoding parameters), so under greedy or low-temperature
decoding $k \approx 0$ (Definition~\ref{def:k}) and the attack costs \emph{one
inference}, independent of $|T|$. Since a verifier must independently recompute
the binding, response binding and response-content hiding are mutually
exclusive. We therefore restate (G2) as what is established---\emph{no plaintext
of $T$ in the public values}, confirmed directly by the leak experiment---and not
as content irrecoverability. The ``content privacy'' cells on \emph{both} sides
must not be read as ``content is unrecoverable''; this is a bound on our own
system first.

\emph{Policy privacy has the same caliber.} The salted commitment closes the
public-value channel, but (i) the default \texttt{core} artefact carries
\texttt{spec\_canonical} and can leak policy and salt together; (ii) the circuit
now coarsens public values (L0 raw, L1 category, L2 opaque), but L0 still names
the rule and the \texttt{core} artefact carries the witness; and (iii) the
measured curve shows L1 still leaks rule categories.
\emph{``Salt added'' is not ``policy privacy solved,''} and ``zero-knowledge''
in the title names the \texttt{gr\-oth16} artefact, not \texttt{core}.

\subsection{Where zero-knowledge pays, and where it does not}
\label{sec:disc-zkmap}

Section~\ref{sec:eval-baseline} measured the non-ZK alternative---a signed
Merkle log---and everything in it is milliseconds, so a proof must earn its cost
against what a signature cannot do. The map has two axes: is the response
secret, and is the policy secret? Zero-knowledge pays only where at least one is
unknown to the verifier. In the transparent corner---public response, public
policy---a signed log certifies the same thing at three to four orders of
magnitude less cost, and a proof is redundancy rather than rigour. The criticism
that ``a signature would have done'' is correct there and wrong in the other
three corners.

One dependency belongs in the open. The zero-knowledge property is a property of
the \emph{wrapper}, not of the system as a whole, and the default tier does not
have it (\S\ref{sec:disc-privacy}). \texttt{gr\-oth16} is now a measured
delivery tier (1{,}874\,B, verified, \S\ref{sec:eval-proofs}), which is why the
title keeps the word; the claim is still wrapper-level, not post-quantum, and it
does not cover the default \texttt{core} artefact. If the wrapper tier is
removed from a deployment, the word must leave the title with it.

\subsection{Availability: the unanchored-pattern cost attack}
\label{sec:disc-dos}

A regular expression with an unanchored pattern---one with no left anchor, such
as \texttt{.*foo}---gives the matcher no way to reject a prefix early: every
position stays a live start until the whole string is read. The prover's work
therefore scales with the response, and whoever \emph{chooses} the response can
drive it quadratically---often the agent itself. This is a denial of service
against the \emph{prover}, not an attack on soundness: no false certificate is
produced and the cost falls on the honest side. It is specific to proving,
because the trace---not merely the verdict---is proved. Mitigations exist and
are partly in place: a Pike VM simulates the NFA in linear time, committing to
response length up front removes the adversary's freedom to inflate it, and
chunked matching bounds per-chunk work.

\emph{Observation (prover-side amplification).} On the proving path the same
family moves the naive baseline from a successful proof at $n=25$
(153.2\,s, 12{,}137\,MiB) to OOM/SIGKILL at $n=50$ (226.5\,s, 13{,}772\,MiB
peak) on a 14.6\,GiB machine, while Pike still proves at $n=100$
(122.2\,s, 11{,}695\,MiB). Because the prover's $\approx$10\,GiB fixed floor
dominates, these wall-clock figures demonstrate amplification and a resource
boundary, not an asymptotic lower bound; the formal statement (Section~S1)
bounds the naive matcher, not the compliance problem.

Two implementation defects surfaced when the matcher was differentially fuzzed
against Python's \texttt{re}; both are fixed in commit \texttt{9ac2d59} and
reported as closed rather than pending. \textbf{G-1}: an empty character class
such as \texttt{[{\textasciicircum}\textbackslash d\textbackslash D]} compiled to
an $\varepsilon$ edge and matched the empty string; the parser now rejects empty
classes at compile time with \texttt{RegexSyntaxError}, making ``non-empty
character class'' a parser contract. \textbf{V1-2}: \texttt{/a\{0\}/} raised
\texttt{AttributeError}; it now compiles to $\varepsilon$, and the Rust naive
matcher's empty-text gap was fixed. V1-2 touched guest code, so the policy vkey
moved from \texttt{0x0091b406\ldots} to \texttt{0x003eb14d\ldots} and
\texttt{pop-script} to \texttt{sha256 7f99fdb1\ldots}; pre-fix results must not
be mixed into a table using the new key. Neither defect broke soundness (V1-2
failed before proving), and the fuzz campaign reported zero mismatches on the
supported subset.

\subsection{Coverage: a provider can simply withhold certificates}
\label{sec:disc-coverage}

Nothing in the cryptographic layer can compel a provider to issue a certificate
at all: a provider that never produces one is indistinguishable, from the
verifier's side, from a provider with nothing to certify. Without trusted
hardware there is no anchor that forces issuance, and the mechanisms that
could---sampling audits, economic penalties, disclosure obligations---live above
the cryptographic layer.

The experiment of \S\ref{sec:eval-issuance} makes the boundary precise.
\emph{Impossibility}: if issuance is optional, a provider that withholds
violates completeness and no proof system can detect it from the certificate
alone. \emph{Conditional sufficiency}: if the operator must append a digest
binding the \emph{delivered} artifact before delivery, and the log is append-only
and publicly readable with inclusion proofs, then issuance completeness holds
for delivered-and-accepted artifacts. \emph{Detection}: an audit over delivered
artifacts finds $0.9948$ of $100$ withheld violations at a $5\%$ sampling rate,
while an audit over log entries finds $0.0000$---the sample frame, not the
sampling luck, is the issue. Three premises travel with the result: (F-1) the
append-before-deliver obligation is external to cryptography; (F-3) the log
entry must bind the delivered $T$ (or a receipt), not only
$H(\mathrm{cert})$; and (F-4) the sample frame is delivered artifacts. The
result quantifies \emph{detection}, never \emph{prevention}.

The log prototype implements RFC~6962-style consistency proofs and a minimal
gossip message, but lacks a gossip \emph{channel} and an authenticated roster,
so split-view resistance is an external assumption: one message detects rollback
and rewriting, but not a same-prefix fork, which requires comparing two heads.

\subsection{Why composition alone does not close the gap}
\label{sec:disc-composition}

The natural next step is to compose: prove inference produced $T$ from the
request, conjoin a proof that $\pi(T)$ holds, and conclude the agent \emph{sent}
a compliant response. The conclusion does not follow for free. A model proof
bound to \emph{some} input shows only that \emph{some} input yields a compliant
output; the gap closes only when the model proof is bound to the \emph{input the
user submitted}, $x$---the same structural role as $f > 0$ in
Theorem~\ref{thm:t3}, a value the adversary cannot choose after seeing the
challenge. This is about \emph{commitment}, not entropy: $x_U$ need not be
uniform or secret and contributes no $f$ fresh bits. The contract is short: the
inference proof must expose $T$ in its public values, bind $x$, and the halves
must use distinct, pinned verification keys.

\begin{proposition}[Composition, weak form as implemented]
\label{thm:composition}
An accepted composite certificate implies
\begin{multline*}
\exists x:\; x=\mathsf{derive}(T_U)\;\wedge\; T_U=M(x)\\
\wedge\;R(\pi,T_U)\;\wedge\; b=B(n,T_U),
\end{multline*}
provided that (C1) both halves are sound, with each delegated rule carrying a
verified companion proof; (C2) both halves are bound to the same fresh $n$;
(C3) the verifier recomputes $B(n,T_U)$ from the delivered $T_U$ and both halves
equal it; and (C5) \texttt{delegated} is empty, or every delegated rule has a
verified companion proof whose public instance contains
$\mathsf{encode}(T_U)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[The strong form is not claimed (E-1)]
\label{rem:e1}
The strong form would replace $x=\mathsf{derive}(T_U)$ by $x=x_U$, the input the
user submitted; that is condition (C4). The current verifier does not receive
$x_U$: it binds the inference half to $\mathsf{derive}(T_U)$. Cell~(c) of
Table~S4 is accepted with \texttt{ok} and \texttt{satisfied} both true while
$x_U\neq\mathsf{derive}(T_U)$. Closing the gap requires putting $x_U$ or a
commitment to it into the public values and recomputing
$\mathsf{IB}(n,x_U)$ during verification. Until then we claim only the weak form
above and mark C4 as undischarged; composition therefore does not close the
divergent-delivery gap by itself.
\end{remark}

Each condition is necessary in the strong form: dropping C1 admits a forged
half, C2 two nonces, C3 two $T$ values under the same $n$, C4 a derived input,
and C5 an undecided delegated rule. The four-cell experiment confirms necessity
cell by cell (Table~S4); cell~(d) is accepted as a certificate but fails the
policy outcome, the intended fail-closed behaviour.

\need{BLOCKER WP4-(2)/WP3 (external resources): real-model scale
($\ge10^6$ parameters) and composition cost data are not measured. The claim
that composition cost is dominated by inference proving is \emph{unverified} at
proxy scale (\texttt{bench/results/compose.md}) and is not made here.}

\subsection{Gateway trust, and a blocked circuit check}
\label{sec:disc-gateway}

The tool-receipt chain is checked for structure inside the circuit, but its
signature is verified off circuit: Ed25519 inside the zkVM is expensive, and the
circuit type deliberately keeps the signature field out of its view. Two
consequences follow. First, the trust root still contains the gateway key: a
compromised gateway can sign receipts that do not match execution; the 2-of-3
threshold prototype weakens that single point of trust but does not remove it,
nor does it close tail truncation, because two gateways can still collaborate on
a truncated chain. Second, the in-circuit cost is \emph{blocked}, not cheap:
measuring it requires a new guest or circuit modification and a machine with
enough memory, neither of which this study had, so the cycle, proving-time and
memory cells are null. We do not convert the $0.094$--$10.165$\,ms off-circuit
figures into an in-circuit estimate; the two carry different trust meanings and
different orders of magnitude.

\subsection{Remaining limitations}
\label{sec:disc-rest}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Two delivery-side properties are assumed, not delivered.}
    In-circuit Ed25519 verification is blocked
    (\S\ref{sec:disc-gateway}), so the gateway remains in the trust root; the
    transparency log lacks a gossip channel and an authenticated roster, so
    split-view resistance is external (\S\ref{sec:disc-coverage}); issuance
    results quantify detection only.
  \item \textbf{Policy privacy is conditional on proving tier and disclosure
    level.} The salt closes the public-value channel only; the \texttt{core}
    artefact remains open, L0 still names the rule, and L1 still leaks rule
    categories. The v1 certificate L2 carries unsalted
    $\mathsf{SHA256}(\text{evidence})$ commitments, which link equal evidence
    across samples (measured $0.382$ on the constructed holdout versus $0.017$
    for count-only, \S\ref{sec:eval-policy}); the v2 circuit L2 salts each
    commitment with the response binding, but the 300-candidate run was not
    repeated on v2, and list length still leaks the violation count.
  \item \textbf{Three-party collusion is undetectable at the signature layer.}
    If policy author, operator and prover all collude, the certificate is
    indistinguishable from an honest one; the multi-prover construction
    partitions responsibility but does not prevent agreement.
  \item \textbf{Signing, key custody and slicing are not production-grade.} The
    on-chain path takes plaintext private-key parameters, the demo uses a public
    Anvil test key, and deployment is local; Ed25519/HSM signing and key custody
    remain future work. Multiple provers hold distinct keys, but that is a
    division of responsibility, not a per-role circuit.
  \item \textbf{Semantic-rule model quality.} The bundled model is a
    demonstration proxy, not a usable learned guardrail. A real 67M-parameter
    DistilBERT toxic-comment classifier on XSTest (450 prompts) is not a
    substitute: at $\theta=0.5$ recall is $0.50\%$, F1 $0.010$, ROC-AUC
    $0.583$, while an all-safe baseline already scores $55.56\%$ accuracy
    against the classifier's $54.89\%$. The mismatch is task-level (toxic text
    versus harmful request), not a threshold defect; we demote the learned
    semantic rule and do not cite XSTest accuracy as support. Its public
    instance also contains $\mathsf{encode}(T)$, so semantic rules are
    \emph{public-mode only}.
  \item \textbf{The inference proof is faithful, not good.} The inference half
    is a deterministic fixed-point MLP proxy whose weights are generated from a
    compile-time seed and baked into the program, hence committed by the
    verification key. It proves that \emph{this} graph computed \emph{this}
    output on \emph{this} response---not that the graph is any good. Swapping it
    for a real prover touches only the prover; the composition layer does not
    move.
  \item \textbf{Verification remains $O(N)$.} There is no recursive aggregation,
    so a verifier pays per certificate; the supported regex subset and its ASCII
    semantics are bounded in the same way, and proof-cost optimisation (lookup
    arguments, parallelism, precomputation) is unexplored.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper began from a small question---what does a compliance certificate
actually bind?---and the answer was the same on all three sides. A proof binds
only the bits submitted into it \emph{and} not selectable by the adversary in
advance. On the response side those are the entropy $k_T$ of $T$ given the
adversary's knowledge; on the policy side $k_\pi$ or the salt; on the request
side the $f$ bits of receiver freshness, without which a certificate is
replayable without limit. Proposition~\ref{thm:t1}, Theorem~\ref{thm:dpriv} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:t3} are three faces of that statement, and
Proposition~\ref{thm:gap}---a gap we state against our own design---is what
makes it bite.

PoP is the constructive instance, not a new proof system. It composes an
existing zkVM, policy compiler and signature layer into a certificate a verifier
can check from public values without trusted hardware---and is explicit about
the limits (\S\ref{sec:discussion}): response content is not unrecoverable, the
default proof artefact is not hidden, and issuance is not forced. These are
limits of the problem, not of one implementation.

The design conclusions are modest and reusable: publish a certificate's leak
profile and audit it like a disclosure policy; let a privacy claim be only as
strong as its arithmetic; put the freshness where the binding is. A certificate
is worth exactly as much as the bits that went into it.
