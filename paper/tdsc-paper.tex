\begin{abstract}
% 决策 E2：内容隐私上界必须出现在摘要，不能埋进 §Limitations。
% 决策 D1：贡献陈述按"分析 x 系统各半"写 —— 分析在前，系统是分析所需的那一实例。
Proof-of-Policy (PoP) lets an auditor check that an LLM agent's response satisfies
a published policy, without trusted hardware and without the operator disclosing
the response in the clear. Our contribution is analytical before it is
constructive. We organise compliance certificates as a
binding--hiding--verifiability (BHV) framework in which one measurable
quantity---the guessing entropy of the bound secret given the adversary's side
information---governs the response side, a receiver-supplied freshness budget
governs the request side, and a policy commitment governs the policy side. A
conditional trilemma shows that, below the adversary's evaluation budget, at
most two of binding, hiding and public recomputability coexist
(Proposition~\ref{thm:trilemma}). A leakage-profile theorem then separates three
channels---public values, proof artefacts, and side information---and explains
why a witness-hiding proof closes one channel and not the others
(Theorem~\ref{thm:leak}). On the policy side we give a D-privacy definition that
fixes the artifact so that response leakage is not misattributed to the policy
(Theorem~\ref{thm:dpriv}), a salted-commitment construction that closes the
public-value channel, and a measured disclosure-level study: policy-inference
top-1 accuracy falls from $0.810$ at rule-name disclosure to $0.073$ at
commitment-only disclosure, the latter at zero audit utility
(\S\ref{sec:eval-policy}). PoP is then presented as the instance these bounds
require---a policy compiler, a
% 决策 D5 条件措辞：若 groth16 未成为交付档，把下行的 "zero-knowledge" 换成 "succinct"，
% 并同步改标题与 §8。
circuit whose witness-hiding tier remains conditional on the proving wrapper,
and a signed certificate layer---measured end to end rather than asserted, with
the leak profile of the certificate reported as a result in its own right.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Scope of privacy.} PoP provides \emph{policy}-zero-knowledge:
the certificate reveals neither the policy evaluation trace nor, in
policy-private mode, the policy text. The upper bound of that claim is stated
here and used throughout: (i) any publicly recomputable binding to $T$ admits a
re-execution attack whenever the adversary knows the prompt and the model, so
$T$ is \emph{not} unrecoverable (\S\ref{sec:binding}); (ii) the default
\texttt{core} proving tier does not hide its witness, so policy privacy is
closed for public values but not for proof artefacts until a witness-hiding tier
is measured (\S\ref{sec:eval}); and (iii) a certificate without
receiver-supplied freshness is replayable without limit (Theorem~\ref{thm:t3}).
We do not claim that the word ``zero-knowledge'' applies unconditionally to the
delivered artefact; \S\ref{sec:disc-zkmap} states the condition under which it
does.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{L}{LM} agents increasingly take actions that carry
con\-se\-quences---\allowbreak{}
customer-facing replies, tool invocations, financial and medical content. Regulation such as the
EU AI Act (Articles~12 and~13) and enterprise governance both require that
compliance be auditable. In practice it can only be taken on trust: logs can be
forged, guardrail claims cannot be checked, and content privacy forbids retaining
the plaintext that would settle the question.

Four lines of work each occupy one corner of this problem without spanning it.
\emph{TEE execution proofs}---Proof-of-Guardrail and its relatives---show that
guardrail code was executed and that inputs and outputs were signed; as that work
itself observes, \emph{execution is not compliance}, and the approach carries
roughly $34\%$ latency overhead together with a hardware dependency.
\emph{TEE governance stacks} (Agentrust, cMCP, cA2A) inherit both the hardware
and the vendor facility. \emph{Formal verification} (Lean-Agent and similar)
requires the entire policy to be formalised by hand, and does not apply to
black-box responses. \emph{ZK regex and PII primitives} (zk-regex~V2, verifiable
document redaction \cite{vdr}, ZK Email) are building blocks; none of them is a
policy-level system.

The nearest work, WITNESS, supports two policy primitives, has no peer-reviewed
paper, and---by its own project page---ships an Apache-2.0 reference
implementation, so ``open source'' is not a differentiator; it also offers no
content privacy. \texttt{@brivora/verify} is not zero-knowledge, and DeepProve
proves the \emph{model computation} layer rather than the \emph{policy
compliance} layer. The combination of a general policy DSL compiled to ZK
constraints, \emph{without} a TEE, with selective disclosure of response content
and a formal security model, is an explicit vacancy. This paper occupies it with
PoP---but the system is not the contribution we lead with. Leading with a system
invites the objection that a signature would do; leading with the analysis is
what makes the system necessary, and it is the analysis that this paper is
organised around.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{What we do \emph{not} claim.} We do not claim that policy proofs
bind nothing: our own system already binds the response through a verifier-issued
nonce (Definition~\ref{def:system}). We do not claim to be the first to observe
that a proof can fail to bind the artifact it purports to describe: the
\emph{effort gap} for ZK inference proofs is formalised by Hollow-LLM
\cite{hollowllm}, and the \emph{source-integrity gap} for predicate proofs is
named by zk-attach \cite{zkattach}. And we do not claim the separation theorem
that our designated-verifier interface makes tempting (``public verification is
impossible when $k$ is small''); Remark~\ref{rem:designated} gives the reason,
and Corollary~\ref{cor:reexec} is the short version of it.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We isolate the gap that survives in every design we surveyed---\emph{divergent
  delivery}---and show that neither hardware nor any proof closes it, while having
  the receiver contribute to the proven statement does
  (Proposition~\ref{thm:gap}).
  \item We give a binding--hiding--verifiability framework in which response,
  request and \emph{policy} are three instances of one accounting
  (\S\ref{sec:tradeoff}--\S\ref{sec:policy-side}). The single guess bound
  $q\cdot\mathsf{Guess}(X\mid Z)$ is standard; what is new is the framework, the
  conditional trilemma with its public-recomputation leg
  (Proposition~\ref{thm:trilemma}), the policy-side instance
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:dpriv}), the necessary conditions for composition
  (\S\ref{sec:disc-composition}), and the measurements.
  \item We price the fix. A certificate is replayable without limit unless the
  receiver contributes fresh randomness, and with an empty nonce the adversary's
  advantage is $1$ regardless of every other parameter of the system
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:t3}).
  \item We describe PoP, a system that realises this design without a TEE
  (\S\ref{sec:system}), and we report what its certificates leak about the policy
  they certify---including a measured inference curve over three disclosure
  levels (\S\ref{sec:eval-policy}).
\end{enumerate}

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{One spine, three sides.} Theorems~\ref{thm:t1}, \ref{thm:t3} and
Proposition~\ref{thm:trilemma} state the same fact about three different inputs:
\emph{a proof binds only as much as the committed, adversary-non-selectable
quantity has bits for}. The response side is governed by entropy,
$k = -\log_2 \mathsf{Guess}(T \mid Z)$ (Definition~\ref{def:k}); the request side
by freshness, $f$; the policy side by the guessing entropy $k_\pi$ of the policy
or by a $128$-bit salt that replaces it. None of the three can be bought with
more computation.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{What we do not rank.} We do not claim that PoP is ``stronger''
than TEE execution proofs. The two prove orthogonal properties---one that a
computation occurred, the other that a property holds---and neither dominates
the other; they compose rather than compete.

\section{System and Threat Model}
\label{sec:system}

The object of study is a \emph{policy-proof system}: a pipeline that compiles a
policy written in a small DSL into a circuit, evaluates an agent's response
against that circuit inside a zkVM, and emits a certificate binding the verdict
to the response. This section fixes the object and its adversary before any
theorem is stated.

\subsection{The system}
\label{sec:system-overview}

\paragraph{Pipeline and the cross-layer contract.} A policy package is compiled
to a \texttt{ConstraintSpec}---a JSON artefact carrying the compiled NFA---which
is the \emph{only} object crossing the layer boundary: the Python reference
implementation and the Rust/zkVM guest consume the same bytes, so cross-layer
drift is structurally impossible rather than merely tested against. The guest
runs a deterministic evaluator and commits its outcome. Of eight rule classes,
seven are decided \emph{inside} the circuit
(\texttt{keyword\_\allowbreak block},
\texttt{normalized\_\allowbreak keyword\_\allowbreak block},
\texttt{length\_\allowbreak bound}, \texttt{pattern\_\allowbreak block},
\texttt{format\_\allowbreak check}, \texttt{tool\_\allowbreak arg\_\allowbreak guard},
\texttt{budget\_\allowbreak bound}). The eighth,
\texttt{semantic\_bound}---a rule of the form ``harm probability $\le$
threshold''---is precisely the kind for which no formal proof can be learned, and
the circuit does not decide it. The circuit registers only \emph{that the rule
was delegated}, in the public value \texttt{delegated}, and a verifier must
\emph{conjunctively} check a companion \texttt{ezkl}/halo2 proof. Verifying the
zkVM side alone does not establish compliance, and we say so here because it is
the most likely misreading of the certificate.

\paragraph{What the verdict is bound to.} Rules that reason about the agent's
\emph{tool calls} cannot be judged against a list the prover fills in itself.
Receipts are therefore issued by a tool gateway after each call and chained
(\texttt{seq}, \texttt{prev}, signature); the circuit checks the chain's
\emph{structure}---entries consecutive, mutually consistent, each digest
recomputed from its own content---and fails closed to \texttt{trace\_unbound}
otherwise. Signature verification happens \emph{off circuit} (Ed25519 inside the
zkVM is prohibitively expensive), a boundary we state rather than gloss: the
circuit is blind to a forgery in the chain's final element, and two independent
off-circuit gates---digest comparison and signature verification---carry that
obligation, neither substituting for the other. Truncating the chain's tail is a
separate and weaker opening, closed by a session-end \texttt{ToolSeal}
commitment from the gateway, and it remains conditional on the verifier holding
the gateway's public key. \S\ref{sec:discussion} develops these residual
boundaries; they are not incidental to the design.

\paragraph{Dual privacy modes.} In \emph{transparent} mode the response and the
verdict are public. In \emph{private} mode the certificate publishes a response
commitment, a commitment per violation, and a redaction proof
(\texttt{redaction\_ok} together with \texttt{mask\_covered}, the latter
requiring the masked positions to fall inside a span the circuit verified to be a
genuine complete match). The guarantee is that \emph{public values contain no
plaintext}---not that $T$ cannot be recovered. A commitment is a public function
of $T$ against which anyone can guess and check, while the response binding must
be publicly recomputable for a verifier to check the string it was delivered.
Those two requirements are in direct tension, and \S\ref{sec:binding} quantifies
exactly when.

\paragraph{Certificates and anchoring.} A certificate is signed with a DSSE
envelope under Ed25519 with the key held by the issuing party, and its digest is
appended to a hash-chain anchor ledger. Two backends share one interface: a file
ledger, verifiable offline, and an on-chain registration storing only a 32-byte
digest. The transaction hash and on-chain timestamp are written back into the
local entry, so the offline chain and the public timestamp cross-check each
other and tampering with either is detectable.

\paragraph{Framework integration.} One gateway serves LangChain callbacks,
LangGraph (\texttt{guard\_node} plus full event streams), and MCP tool argument
and response checks, so trace certificates and proofs share a single chain of
evidence. The implementation passes 801 tests with 15 skipped; the skipped set is
enumerated in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

\begin{definition}[Policy-proof system]
\label{def:system}
A policy-proof system is a tuple $(\mathsf{Prove}, \mathsf{Ver}, \pi, \mathcal{M})$.
On challenge $n$ from a verifier, $\mathsf{Prove}(\pi, T, n)$ outputs a
certificate $c$ whose public values include
\begin{multline*}
b \;=\; H(\texttt{pop-bind-v1} \,\|\, \mathsf{u32be}(|n|) \,\|\, n \,\|\, T),\\
C = H(T).
\end{multline*}
$\mathsf{Ver}(c, n, T')$ accepts iff the policy relation holds \emph{and}
$b$ recomputed from the received $T'$ matches.
\end{definition}

The relation does contain a term referring to the delivered bytes: $b$ is
recomputed from the received $T'$ at verification. An earlier draft of this
work claimed the opposite; Definition~\ref{def:system} is the corrected form,
and the correction matters because it is what makes
Proposition~\ref{thm:gap} a statement about \emph{where} the proved string is
delivered rather than about whether it is bound at all.

\subsection{Threat model}
\label{sec:threat}

The adversary is the \emph{agent operator}: the party that holds the policy, runs
the model, and produces the certificate. The verifier is a regulator, auditor, or
third party holding only public values. The policy author issues $\pi$; in this
section we treat it as honest, and \S\ref{sec:wp4-tension} makes that assumption
explicit and scopes it, because in policy-private mode its honesty is doing real
work and cannot be waved through. We state the goals, and more importantly the
non-goals, before the theorems.

\begin{assumption}[Adversary side information]
\label{as:Z}
The adversary knows the prompt, the model (possibly its weights), and the
decoding parameters. \emph{This is the assumption that makes the theorem
non-trivial}: under greedy decoding, $k \approx 0$.
\end{assumption}

\paragraph{Goals.}
\begin{description}
  \item[(G1)] \emph{Soundness}---a response $T$ violating $\pi$ cannot yield an
  accepted certificate.
  \item[(G2)] \emph{Committed privacy}---in private mode the public values
  contain no plaintext of $T$. This is explicitly \emph{not} the claim that $T$
  is unrecoverable: once the guessing entropy $k$ of Definition~\ref{def:k} falls
  below $\log q + \log(1/\varepsilon)$, a publicly recomputable binding and
  content hiding are mutually exclusive (Theorem~\ref{thm:t1}).
  \item[(G3)] \emph{Traceable binding}---policy version, circuit, and proof
  artefact are bound to one another and recomputed at verification.
  \item[(G4)] \emph{Selective disclosure}---redaction and evidence opening are
  themselves provable.
  \item[(G5)] \emph{Record integrity}---tampering with, or reordering, the audit
  ledger is detectable.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Non-goals.} We do not prove the model's inference, and we do not cover
training data or model cards. (G1) is modulo the soundness of the zkVM and of the
hash function; we use those primitives and do not re-derive them.

\paragraph{Where the policy-private case is developed.} The three-party model,
the issuing prerequisite (P-PP), and the table of what each verifier may and may
not conclude are specific to policy-private mode, where the policy itself is
secret. They are stated once, in \S\ref{sec:wp4-tension}, and are deliberately
\emph{not} duplicated here---in the space available they cannot be. The general
adversary of this section is the same operator, viewed without the
policy-secrecy refinement.

\section{Binding of Policy Proofs}
\label{sec:binding}

A policy proof is meant to bind a response: a verifier who accepts a
certificate should conclude that \emph{this} string was checked against the
policy. This section asks what that binding is actually worth. We first isolate
the gap that survives in every design we surveyed (\S\ref{sec:gap}), then give
a quantitative bound on what a public value reveals, together with the
structural hypothesis that bound needs (\S\ref{sec:tradeoff}), and finally
state the same fact on the request side, where the governing resource is
freshness rather than entropy (\S\ref{sec:t3}).

\subsection{The gap}
\label{sec:gap}

\begin{proposition}[Divergent delivery]
\label{thm:gap}
Definition \ref{def:system} binds $T'$ \emph{as received by the verifying party}. If the
provider delivers a different string $T'' \ne T'$ to the end user, verification
still succeeds on $T'$. Neither hardware nor a proof can prevent this; only the
end user holding $n$ and recomputing $b$ can.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$b$ is a function of $(n,T)$ only; the delivery channel to the end user carries
no authenticated term. One line.
\end{proof}

Two remarks scope this proposition. First, it describes a \emph{residual} gap,
not the claim that policy proofs bind nothing. Our own system already binds $T$
through the verifier-issued nonce $n$ of Definition \ref{def:system}, and a
verifier who receives $T'$ and recomputes $b$ does learn that $T'$ was the
proved string; the gap is that the binding is anchored to the
\emph{verifying party's} copy. Second, we do not claim to be the first to
observe that a proof can fail to bind the artifact it purports to describe. The
\emph{effort gap} for zero-knowledge inference proofs is formalized by
Hollow-LLM \cite{hollowllm}, and the \emph{source-integrity gap} for predicate
proofs is named by zk-attach \cite{zkattach}; both concern what the proof system
was asked to prove, whereas Proposition \ref{thm:gap} concerns where the proved
string is delivered. We state the naive version of our own claim because it
would be false against our own system.

\subsection{A binding--hiding--verifiability framework}
\label{sec:tradeoff}

A certificate must publish \emph{something} tying it to the secret it certifies;
otherwise it binds nothing at all. We ask what that public value leaks, and we
ask the question uniformly for the response $T$, the policy $\pi$, and the
challenge on the request side. The answer depends on one measurable quantity,
the guessing entropy, and on one structural property: whether the binding can be
recomputed by anyone without a secret input.

\begin{definition}[Guessing entropy]
\label{def:k}
For a secret distribution $\mathcal{D}$ and adversary side information $Z$, let
\begin{multline*}
\mathsf{Guess}(X \mid Z) \;\triangleq\; \max_{\mathcal{A}} \Pr[\mathcal{A}(Z) = X]\\
\;=\; \mathbb{E}_z\!\left[\max_x \Pr[X = x \mid Z = z]\right],
\end{multline*}
\[
k_X \;\triangleq\; -\log_2 \mathsf{Guess}(X \mid Z),
\]
the maximum ranging over all time-unbounded adversaries.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[The definition is operational, and it is the average]
\label{rem:kmeas}
$k_X$ is \emph{measured}, not assumed: sampling one model on one prompt many
times estimates $\mathsf{Guess}(T \mid Z)$ directly (\S\ref{sec:eval-k}). The
definition must average over $z$ rather than take the pointwise worst case
$k_z = -\log \max_t \Pr[T = t \mid Z = z]$: the proof takes an expectation over
$z$, and $\mathbb{E}_z[2^{-k_z}] = 2^{-k_X}$ holds only for the average form.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Two strengths of public verifiability.} \emph{(PV-1)} asks that some
PPT verifier, holding public values and no secret state, check the proof.
\emph{(PV-2)} asks that there be a PPT algorithm $E$ with no secret input such
that $E(X)=\beta(X)$ for every $X$: anyone can recompute the binding offline and
compare it with the delivered artifact. The distinction is not pedantic. A keyed
binding $\beta_s(X)=H(s,X)$ can retain PV-1---a zero-knowledge proof still
verifies---while losing PV-2, because a verifier without $s$ cannot recompute
$\beta_s$. The trilemma below is a statement about PV-2. Our response binding
$b=H(\texttt{pop-bind-v1}\|n\|T)$ satisfies PV-2; a salted policy commitment
does not.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional trilemma]
\label{thm:t1}
\label{thm:trilemma}
Fix $\lambda,\varepsilon>0$ and $q\ge 1$. Let $X$ be a secret with guessing
entropy $k_X<\log q+\log(1/\varepsilon)$ whose binding is \emph{guess-tight}:
$\beta_\rho(X)=H(\mathsf{DOM}\|\rho\|X)$ for a random oracle $H$ and public
randomness $\rho$ independent of $X$ and $Z$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(1)] \emph{Public binding.} For any adversary that evaluates $\beta$ at
most $q$ times,
\begin{multline*}
\Pr\!\left[\mathcal{A}^\beta(Z,\beta(X))=X\right]\\
\le q\cdot\mathsf{Guess}(X\mid Z)+\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)
= q\cdot 2^{-k_X}+\mathrm{negl}(\lambda).
\end{multline*}
Hiding with advantage $\varepsilon$ therefore needs
$k_X\ge\log q+\log(1/\varepsilon)$. The integer $q$ counts evaluations of
$\beta$, not time: the adversary may be computationally unbounded.
\item[(2)] \emph{Keyed binding.} For $\beta_s(X)=H(s,X)$ with $s$ held only by a
designated party and absent from the public values,
$\Pr[\mathcal{A}(Z,b)=X]\le\mathsf{Guess}(X\mid Z)+\mathrm{negl}(\kappa)$.
The correct reading is \emph{not} ``hiding survives even when $k_X=0$'': if
$k_X=0$ then $Z$ already determines $X$, and no binding can help. What the keyed
form buys is hiding when $k_X<\log q+\log(1/\varepsilon)$, i.e.\ when the public
form would fall to enumeration. Its price is PV-2.
\item[(3)] \emph{Conditional trilemma.} Consider schemes whose binding is
publicly recomputable (PV-2), whose hiding is exact recovery, and whose binding
is guess-tight. When $k_X<\log q+\log(1/\varepsilon)$, such a scheme cannot
satisfy binding, hiding and PV-2 simultaneously: public recomputability gives
(1), and avoiding (1) requires a keyed binding, which forfeits PV-2.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
(1) Fix $z$ and decompose by the first query that hits $X$. Conditioned on no
earlier hit, every answer already seen is uniform and independent of $X$, so
each query succeeds with probability at most $2^{-k_z}$; taking the expectation
over $z$ and using $\mathbb{E}_z[2^{-k_z}]=\mathsf{Guess}(X\mid Z)$ gives
$q\cdot 2^{-k_X}$. The random-oracle idealisation and the SHA-256 instantiation
are collected in $\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$; if $q$ is not assumed polynomial the
term $q\cdot 2^{-\lambda}$ must be kept explicitly rather than absorbed.
(2) Without an oracle for $\beta_s$, the value $b$ is uniform and independent of
$X$ in the random-oracle model, so the best strategy is the prior mode; an
enumeration also has to guess $s$, at cost $2^{-\kappa}$. (3) The three legs are
excluded in turn as stated. Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} gives the full argument.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[A classification, not an impossibility theorem]
\label{rem:trilemma-scope}
Proposition~\ref{thm:t1}(3) is a \emph{conditional classification}: its
hypotheses are PV-2, exact-recovery hiding, a guess-tight binding, and a
near-uniform candidate set. It does not prove that binding, hiding and public
verification are mutually exclusive under every verification model. The escapes
are real and we name them: PV-1 with a keyed binding plus a zero-knowledge proof
(trust for the binding moves to the authority); trusted hardware; an interactive
or designated verifier; a high-entropy secret, where the condition does not
trigger; a relaxed, indistinguishability-based hiding goal; and $k_X=0$, where
hiding has already failed and the trilemma degenerates to ``binding and PV-2
without hiding''. For a skewed distribution the bound certifies only that
\emph{this upper bound cannot certify} $\varepsilon$-hiding; it does not exhibit
a matching attack.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The guess-tight hypothesis is necessary]
\label{rem:betanecessary}
Without it, part (1) is false rather than merely unproven: for
$\beta=\mathrm{id}$ or $\beta=\mathsf{encode}(T)$ the adversary outputs
$\beta(T)$ and wins with probability $1$, independently of $q$ and $k_X$. For an
arbitrary deterministic public $\beta$ the correct bound is over the preimage
class,
\[
\Pr\!\left[\mathcal{A}^\beta(Z,\beta(T))=T\right]\;\le\;
\mathbb{E}_y\!\left[\max_t \Pr[T=t \mid Z,\beta(T)=y]\right],
\]
which may equal $1$. Our own two public values straddle the hypothesis---$b$ is
guess-tight, $\mathsf{encode}(T)$ is not---and that is what makes the pair a
statement about \emph{our design} rather than a citation of a standard bound. An
earlier draft stated (1) without its hypothesis; the counterexample above is why
the hypothesis is now explicit.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Re-execution, not enumeration]
\label{cor:reexec}
Under Assumption~\ref{as:Z}, when decoding is greedy or low-temperature,
$k_T\approx 0$ and the attack of Proposition~\ref{thm:t1} is realised by
\emph{re-running the model}, at the cost of one inference---independent of $|T|$
and requiring \emph{no} evaluation of $\beta$. Enumeration over the string space
is \emph{not} the relevant attack: $2^{100}$ is as unenumerable as $2^{256}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[A design observation, not a separation]
\label{rem:designated}
Our verification interface is \emph{designated}: binding is checked against a
value $b=H(\mathsf{dom}\|n\|T)$ in which a verifier-issued nonce $n$ appears. It
is tempting to promote this into a separation (``public verification is
impossible when $k$ is small''). \textbf{We claim no such theorem, and
Corollary~\ref{cor:reexec} is why.} When $k\approx 0$, hiding fails for
\emph{every} scheme, designated or not, because the adversary re-runs the model;
when $k$ is large, a zero-knowledge proof of
``$\exists T: b=H(\mathsf{dom}\|n\|T)\wedge R(\pi,T)=1$'' is simultaneously
publicly verifiable and hiding. The dichotomy is governed by $k$ alone and
carries no independent content. An earlier draft asserted the separation as a
theorem; we record the demotion because a referee would otherwise find the
counterexample in one line.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The leakage profile: one theorem, three regimes}
\label{sec:leak}

Proposition~\ref{thm:t1} assumed that the certificate depends on $X$ only
through its public binding. A real certificate also ships a proof artefact, and
the verifier has prior information. The following decomposition makes the three
contributions explicit.

\begin{theorem}[Leakage-profile decomposition]
\label{thm:leak}
Let $V=(L_{\mathrm{pub}},L_{\mathrm{proof}},L_{\mathrm{side}})$ be the verifier's
view, where $L_{\mathrm{pub}}$ is the certificate's public values,
$L_{\mathrm{proof}}$ is what the proof artefact reveals about the witness
$w=(\pi,T,\mathrm{trace})$, and $L_{\mathrm{side}}$ is the adversary's prior
information $Z$. For any $q\ge 1$ and any adversary that evaluates $\beta$ at
most $q$ times,
\begin{multline*}
\Pr\!\left[\mathcal{A}^\beta(V)=X\right]\\
\;\le\; q\cdot\mathsf{Guess}\!\left(X\mid L_{\mathrm{pub}},L_{\mathrm{proof}},L_{\mathrm{side}}\right)
+\mathrm{negl}(\lambda),
\end{multline*}
with the final output charged as one evaluation.
\end{theorem}

The inequality itself is close to the definition of guessing entropy when
$q\ge1$: an adversary without a $\beta$ oracle has $\Pr[\mathcal{A}(V)=X]\le
\mathsf{Guess}(X\mid V)$. The content of Theorem~\ref{thm:leak} is the
\emph{decomposition} in Table~\ref{tab:regimes}, which turns one bound into
three claims about three proving regimes. In particular, a witness-hiding proof
sets $L_{\mathrm{proof}}=\bot$ and reduces the theorem to the public-value bound;
it does not touch $L_{\mathrm{side}}$.

\begin{proposition}[The lossy instance]
\label{prop:semantic}
For the semantic rule class, the public values contain
$\beta(T)=\mathsf{encode}(T)$, a content-preserving feature vector of the
response. This $\beta$ is deterministic and injective on features but not on
strings, so it violates the guess-tight hypothesis and instantiates the
preimage-class bound: the binding force is exactly
$\mathsf{Guess}(T\mid Z,\mathsf{encode}(T))$. The adversary's search space is the
preimage of the published vector, not the string space; the relevant asymmetry
is a \emph{lossy public encoding}, not ``low entropy''.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{The policy side: D-privacy and a salted commitment}
\label{sec:policy-side}

The same accounting applies to the policy. The policy text is a witness; only
$\mathsf{policy\_hash}=H(\mathsf{canonical}(\pi))$ is public. But that hash is a
public, offline-recomputable function of $\pi$---the same mathematical object as
$b$---so ``the policy is a witness'' closes the \emph{text} channel and not the
\emph{hash} channel. Before stating the privacy goal we fix a defect in the
naive formulation.

\paragraph{The artifact must be fixed.} If the two worlds may use different
artifacts $T_0\neq T_1$, the verifier distinguishes them by computing $B(n,T_0)$
and $B(n,T_1)$ once. That is response-side leakage, not policy leakage.
Accordingly the game fixes one artifact $T$ and one challenge $n$, or
equivalently requires the two artifacts to have the same public $T$-view; only
$\pi_0$ and $\pi_1$ differ.

\begin{definition}[D-privacy]
\label{def:dpriv}
Let $D:\Pi\to\mathbb{D}$ be the disclosure function. A scheme is
$D$-private if for every PPT adversary, every $(\pi_0,\pi_1,T,n)$ with
$D(\pi_0)=D(\pi_1)$ and $R(\pi_0,T)=R(\pi_1,T)$, the views
$\mathsf{View}_b=(L_{\mathrm{pub}}(\pi_b,T,n),L_{\mathrm{proof}}(\pi_b,T,n),
L_{\mathrm{side}})$ for $b\in\{0,1\}$ are computationally indistinguishable.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[D-privacy from three conditions]
\label{thm:dpriv}
The scheme is $D$-private if (C1) there is a PPT simulator $S_{\mathrm{rest}}$
such that, for every admissible $(\pi,T,n)$, the public values other than the
commitment satisfy
$(h_\pi,S_{\mathrm{rest}}(pp,h_\pi,D(\pi),\mathrm{pub}_T,n))\approx_c
L_{\mathrm{pub}}(\pi,T,n)$; (C2) the proof system is witness-hiding, i.e.\
$L_{\mathrm{proof}}$ has a simulator that takes only the public statement;
and (C3) the policy commitment is hiding for all $D$-equal policies,
$(pp,h_{\pi_0})\approx_c(pp,h_{\pi_1})$. The advantage is
$O(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pub}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{wh}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{com}})$,
by a five-hybrid argument (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}).
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{The salted construction.} To satisfy (C3), the authority samples a
$128$-bit nonce $s$ per policy package and commits to the canonical bytes with
$s$ as an explicit field,
\[
h_\pi=\mathsf{SHA256}\bigl(\mathsf{canonical}(\pi,s)\bigr),\qquad
s\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{128},
\]
where $\mathsf{canonical}$ is the sorted, compact JSON encoding of the compiled
constraints with \texttt{policy\_nonce} set to $s$. The nonce enters the
\texttt{ConstraintSpec} bytes and therefore the public \texttt{policy\_hash}; it
does \emph{not} enter the certificate's \texttt{policy.id}/\texttt{version}
fields, so the verifier does not see it. The authority registers $h_\pi$ in the
issuing record and opens $(\pi,s)$ during an audit, where the verifier recomputes
$h_\pi$. Without $s$, an enumeration over candidate policies must also guess the
salt: the work is multiplied by $2^{128}$, and the qualitative statement is all
we claim---we do not assert an exact exponent of the form $2^{-(\lambda+128)}$.

\paragraph{What the salt does not close.} Theorem~\ref{thm:dpriv} is
conditional, and two conditions fail in the current system. (C1) fails when the
public values contain \texttt{PrivateViolation\{rule,kind\}} or a non-empty
\texttt{delegated}: both depend on the undisclosed structure of $\pi$, so a
disclosure level $D$ that does not cover them is not a $D$-privacy level at all.
(C2) fails at the default \texttt{core} tier: the trace carries
\texttt{spec\_canonical}, which contains both $\pi$ and the salt, so a
witness-bearing artifact recovers $s$ and then $\pi$ by enumeration. The salt
closes the public-value channel only; it is not ``policy privacy solved,'' and
\S\ref{sec:eval} measures the disclosure-level channel that remains open.

\subsection{Quantitative request binding}
\label{sec:t3}

\begin{theorem}[Delivery binding]
\label{thm:t3}
Fix a policy $\pi$. A delivery adversary $\mathcal{B}$ may, \emph{before}
seeing the receiver's challenge, precompute a set $S$ of accepted transcripts,
where each transcript covers at most $c$ challenges. The receiver then
contributes $f$ fresh, uniformly random bits $r$ that enter the proven
statement. Under the certificate's soundness (D1) and challenge binding
(D2/B-bind), the divergence advantage satisfies
\[
\mathsf{Adv}_{\mathrm{del}}(\mathcal{B}) \;\le\; |S| \cdot c \cdot 2^{-f}
\;+\; \mathrm{negl}(\lambda).
\]
The replay-only probability---acceptance without requiring divergence---is at
most $|S|\cdot c\cdot 2^{-f}$. If the receiver contributes no fresh randomness
($f = 0$; e.g.\ an empty nonce), a precomputed transcript is accepted with
probability $1$ and \emph{does not decay in any parameter of the system};
divergence in that case is bounded only by the binding failure
$\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The receiver's freshness $r$ enters the proven statement through
$b = H(\mathsf{dom} \,\|\, r \,\|\, T)$. For a certificate in $S$ to be accepted
on $r$, the value $r$ must fall inside that certificate's challenge set
$A(\mathrm{cert})$, with $|A(\mathrm{cert})|\le c$; a union bound over $S$ gives
$\sum_{\mathrm{cert}\in S}|A(\mathrm{cert})|/2^f\le |S|\cdot c\cdot 2^{-f}$.
A certificate minted \emph{after} $r$ is seen can diverge only by exhibiting two
distinct preimages of the same $b$ under $B(r,\cdot)$, which is exactly a
challenge-binding failure and costs $\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$. When $f = 0$ every
element of $S$ is a permanently valid replay, so the replay probability is $1$
and does not decay in any parameter of the system. The $f = 0$ branch is not
hypothetical: \texttt{circuits/\allowbreak types/\allowbreak src/\allowbreak
lib.rs} (lines 950--995) documents an empty nonce as a legal input that
``provides no replay protection.''
\end{proof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:t3} completes the spine. It is the request-side instance of the
same accounting that Proposition~\ref{thm:t1} and Theorem~\ref{thm:dpriv} apply
to the response and the policy: a proof binds only as much as the committed
quantity the adversary cannot select in advance has bits for. On the response
side that quantity is $T$ and the resource is entropy $k_T$; on the policy side
it is $\pi$ and the resource is $k_\pi$ or the $128$-bit salt; on the request
side it is freshness $f$. None of the three can be bought with more computation.
The $c$ factor is what the earlier formulation omitted: one certificate may cover
several challenges, so a precomputed set of size $|S|$ can purchase $|S|\cdot c$
attempts, not $|S|$.

\begin{remark}[A taxonomy, not a trichotomy]
\label{rem:taxonomy}
Every policy-proof deployment we surveyed closes the divergent-delivery gap of
Proposition \ref{thm:gap} in one of three ways:
(a) root trust in hardware (attested enclaves);
(b) have the \emph{end user} contribute a secret to the proven statement (the
user issues the nonce); or
(c) gate delivery on successful verification (\emph{prove-before-deliver}).
\textbf{We offer this as a classification, not as an exhaustiveness theorem}:
we neither prove that a fourth family cannot exist, nor that the three are
disjoint. zk-attach \cite{zkattach} walks (a); NiyamAI and ZKAP walk (c); PoP
walks (b).
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Posture, stated plainly.} Our system already carries
\texttt{response\_binding}, but $n$ is issued by the \emph{verifier}. Moving it
to the \emph{end user}, so that $f > 0$ becomes the receiver's contribution,
closes the divergent-delivery branch of Proposition \ref{thm:gap}. We are
explicit that this is a small protocol amendment rather than a new system: the
mechanism is already implemented, and only the issuer of the nonce changes. What
Theorem \ref{thm:t3} buys is the \emph{price}: with $f = 0$ there is no closure
at any cost.

\section{Policy-Private Compliance}
\label{sec:private-policy}

For a policy whose rules are deterministic and a response that is public
anyway, a signed hash chain over the response suffices: the signature already
binds the bytes, and anyone can re-evaluate the rules. The one scenario a
signature cannot cover is a policy that must itself stay secret---where the
operator has to certify compliance to a third party without handing over the
rules it is being judged against. That is the mode this section is about, and we
lead with what the mode \emph{cannot} buy, because that determines whether it
means anything at all.

\subsection{Two channels, not one}
\label{sec:wp4-finding}

\texttt{spec\_canonical} is already a \emph{guest input}---a witness---rather
than a public value; only its hash,
\[
\mathsf{policy\_hash} \;=\; \mathsf{SHA256}(\texttt{spec\_canonical}),
\]
enters the public values (\texttt{circuits/\allowbreak types/\allowbreak
src/\allowbreak lib.rs}:1--16, 415--416, 918, 1003--1004). It is tempting to
conclude that the policy text is already hidden. It is not: the instruction
``do not reveal the policy text'' closes the \emph{text} channel, while
$\mathsf{policy\_hash}$ remains a public, offline-recomputable function of the
policy---the same mathematical object as $b$, and therefore the same enumeration
target. The distinction is not cosmetic: the policy space is small (eight rule
classes, bounded parameters, three to eight rules), so a candidate policy can be
compiled and matched against the published hash.

Two further channels survive as well. The proof artefact is a function of the
witness at the default \texttt{core} tier, and the certificate's public values
contain fields derived from the undisclosed structure of $\pi$. Table~\ref{tab:wp4}
states the channels and what closes each; the salted commitment of
\S\ref{sec:policy-side} closes only the first, and we say so there.

\subsection{Disclosure levels and the inference they permit}
\label{sec:eval-policy}

If the policy cannot be hidden behind a hash alone, the practical question is
how much of it to disclose. We implemented three certificate-layer projections:
L0 exposes the rule name and kind per violation; L1 exposes the rule category,
the per-category count and the total; L2 exposes only the violation count, with
commitments left opaque. These are certificate-layer projections, not
circuit-level coarsening: the guest still writes the raw rule and kind into its
public values, so a party that reads the proof public values directly sees L0
regardless of the certificate level. That boundary is stated again in
Sec.~\ref{sec:disc-policy}.

variants, using 40 training and 15 test responses per variant and a standard
multinomial naive Bayes classifier (seed 20261101). Features are drawn only from
the certificate public values of each level; the random-guess baseline over 20
classes is $0.050$. Table~\ref{tab:policy-infer} reports the result.

channel only; it does not cover the circuit public values (still raw at L0), the
\texttt{core} proof artefact, or the re-execution channel. The classifier is a
stdlib implementation, not an attack upper bound: a stronger classifier can only
raise the L0/L1 numbers, not make the L2 commitments interpretable. And the
numbers are recovery rates on a fixed 20-variant corpus; they estimate the shape
of the curve, not a deployment-specific risk.

The structural lesson is the one Table~\ref{tab:wp4} states: the salt closes the
hash channel, the disclosure level governs the certificate channel, and neither
closes the proof-artefact channel. ``Policy privacy solved'' would require all
three, and the third is still blocked on a witness-hiding tier
(\S\ref{sec:eval-proofs}).

\subsection{The tension worth writing up: policy privacy vs.\ accountability}
\label{sec:wp4-tension}

\paragraph{The prerequisite, stated as a prerequisite.} Let $h_\pi$ be the
policy commitment. On its own $h_\pi$ is \emph{inert}. A prover can always
emit a self-consistent certificate for any policy $\pi'$ it prefers, simply by
placing $h_{\pi'}$ in that certificate. \emph{Secrecy of $\pi$ is what creates
the gap}, and no cryptographic mechanism can close it: closing it would require
showing $\pi$ to the verifier, which is precisely what the mode exists to avoid.
What closes it is an \emph{issuing act}, so we state it as an explicit
prerequisite of the mode.

\begin{description}
  \item[(P-PP)] An \emph{issuing authority} signs $\pi$, registers $h_\pi$ in a
  record readable by third parties, and holds the salt. A policy is
  \emph{accountable} iff its \texttt{policy\_hash} can be traced to such a
  record.
\end{description}

Without (P-PP) the mode is not merely weaker---the verifier's conclusion is
empty. This is the same assumption as the ``honest policy author'' of
\texttt{docs/security-model.md}~\S0.2; what is new is scoping it to the
verification interface, which had not been done, and which a referee would
otherwise use to dismiss the whole mode in one sentence.

\paragraph{Three parties.} ``Three'' means \emph{authority / operator / third
party}; an auditor is a named instance of the third party, not a fourth trust
anchor. Table~\ref{tab:wp4-parties} states the model.

(the leaks in Table~\ref{tab:wp4}, items~4 and~6) breaks secrecy on that
fragment. Second, accountability for $V^{*}$ is \emph{voluntary}: $V^{*}$ can
check only when $O$ chooses to disclose $\pi$ and the salt. The mode offers no
forced disclosure; that would require the release-gating family discussed in
\texttt{docs/tdsc-plan.md}~\S1.6(b), which we do not claim.

\subsection{Cycle count: length versus rule count}
\label{sec:eval-cycles}

Response length was pushed from 200 to 100{,}000 characters and the rule count
from 1 to 6, giving 40 measurement points (\texttt{bench/bench\_cycles.py}; the
full matrix is in \texttt{bench/results/cycles.md}). The response text comes from
an evaluation artefact the repository's own end-to-end demo produced (372
characters, three segments deduplicated by content); longer points are tiled from
it. What is measured is therefore the \emph{curve} of scanning cost, not ``a real
100{,}000-character document''. Every point was first confirmed to match no rule:
a matching rule short-circuits, and the cost of a short-circuit says nothing.

\texttt{bench/results/cycles.md}.}
\label{tab:cycles}
\tabfont
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Rules & \texttt{keyword} & \texttt{pattern} & Slope (cyc/char) & Intercept \\

1 & 0 & 0 & 73.9      & 50{,}137 \\

Three findings, in decreasing order of how much they change what may be claimed.

\emph{(1) With the rule set fixed, cycle count is exactly linear in length.}
Five rule sets, six length points each, $R^2 \approx 1.0000$ throughout
(Table~\ref{tab:cycles}).

\emph{(2) Scanning cost is dominated by the NFA, not by the rule count.} One
keyword rule costs about $+31.6$ cycles per character at the margin; one NFA
regular expression (an email pattern) costs about $+3{,}634$---roughly
$115\times$ more. Equivalently, a rule set containing a regular expression has a
per-character slope ($4{,}289.8$) $58\times$ that of a rule set with only string
rules ($73.9$).

\emph{(3) Unit prices are not additive: a unit price is a property of a rule
\emph{in context}, not of the rule.} Fitting the slopes as
$(\text{keyword},\text{pattern})$ unit prices gives $R^2 = 0.9169$ and a
\emph{negative} keyword unit price ($-477.2$ cycles per character). This is not
noise; two probes give the mechanism directly:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ordering probe} (same rules, same text, $L = 10^5$): declared
  order costs $541{,}342{,}069$ cycles against $570{,}736{,}989$ in reverse,
  a difference of $+5.43\%$, while \texttt{passed} is identical in both. \emph{The
  semantics of the decision do not depend on order; the cost does.}
  \item \textbf{Marginal probe} ($L = 10^5$, filling the one matrix cell the
  sweep could not reach---regular expressions with no keyword rule):
  $[\text{len},\text{pat}]$ over $[\text{len}]$ has a marginal unit price of
  $3{,}633.7$, whereas ``add one more keyword rule'' has two different answers:
  $+31.6$ alone, $+585.6$ in a policy that already contains a regular expression
  ($-153.7$ if inserted before it). The same action changes sign between
  $-154$ and $+586$.
\end{itemize}

The mechanism lies in zkVM memory allocation: every \texttt{ascii\_lower} and
every Pike VM match allocates, and allocator state depends on previous
allocations. We therefore quote \emph{per-rule-set slopes} (the table above,
directly citable) rather than any ``unit price $\times$ count'' expression. The
simple model $\text{cycles} \approx a\cdot|T|\cdot\text{rules} + b$ that this
work anticipated is refuted by the data ($R^2 = 0.872$, negative intercept), and
we report that too: \emph{a model refuted by its own data is itself an evaluation
result.}

\subsection{Matcher ablation: quadratic degradation on a pathological input}
\label{sec:eval-ablation}

decides in a single linear pass (\texttt{bench/bench\_ablation.py}).
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports both paths.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A fixed floor of $\approx$10.15\,GiB.} The smallest
  configuration---200 characters, one rule---already peaks at 10{,}389\,MB: the
  prover's own overhead (the trace and permutation structures of a \texttt{core}
  proof) is almost independent of the trace. All four successes fall inside the
  narrow band 10{,}389--10{,}506\,MB. Length and rule count have little marginal
  effect on memory---\emph{but the headroom is too thin for that to help}.
  \item \textbf{A two-step staircase, not a slope.} One rule is provable up to
  10k characters; two rules at about 200 characters; \textbf{three or more do not
  come out at all}. The third rule in the sweep is a \texttt{pattern\_block}: a
  regular-expression match activates another family of AIR chips and lifts the
  trace area in one step, so the cliff is at $2 \to 3$ rules; in length it is at
  $10\text{k} \to 20\text{k}$.
  \item \textbf{Memory binds, not CPU.} The cycle sweep of
  \S\ref{sec:eval-cycles} reaches 100k characters with 6 rules because it only
  executes in the zkVM and produces no proof; at 100k characters the cycle count
  is still on the order of $10^7$, which any CPU handles. \emph{The proving-side
  ceiling and the execution-side ceiling are in different places.} ``How large a
  policy can be proved'' is, on this machine, a fixed floor plus two steps rather
  than a cost curve.
  \item \textbf{Artefact size is nearly independent of configuration}: from 200
  to 10{,}000 characters the proof grows only from 2716.4 to 2726.6\,KiB. Pure
  verification takes $\approx$90\,ms (the \texttt{vkey} setup is 1.5\,s, once).
  \item \textbf{Reproducibility, stated precisely.} The \emph{position} of the
  wall reproduces (each failing point failed twice); the \emph{timings} at the
  wall do not. Re-running one group in isolation shows $\pm 10\%$ jitter in time
  and $\pm 1\%$ in memory, so we quote time only to an order of magnitude.
  \item \textbf{Session and multiparty.} The session aggregation proof (three
  certificates) measured 152.5\,s; multiparty verification needs one proof
  \emph{per non-empty slice}, so its cost grows linearly in the number of slices
  (a two-proof example policy). Neither introduces a new proving system---a slice
  and a session are ordinary inputs to the guest---but both are subject to the
  same floor: every additional proof adds another $\approx$10\,GiB peak, so
  multiparty proving must run in separate processes, serially.
\end{itemize}

Two caveats belong with Table~\ref{tab:baseline}. The $2.131$\,ms append is a
\emph{static tree recomputation} in \texttt{proofs/session.py}, $\Theta(n)$, not
the amortised $\Theta(\log n)$ of an RFC-6962 incremental log; and the
measurements come from a shared machine with $\pm 20\%$ drift. Source:
\texttt{bench/results/baseline.md}.

The asymmetry is the point, and it is what this arm contributes. The verification
chain is \emph{verify signature} $\to$ \emph{verify log inclusion} $\to$
\emph{re-run $J(\pi,T)$}. The first two steps need only public data; the third
needs the plaintext of both $\pi$ and $T$. That is not an implementation defect
but a structural consequence of the signature paradigm: a signature binds
\emph{who said it} to the bytes, never \emph{whether it is true}. We measured the
consequence rather than asserting it---\textbf{the same key signs a certificate
whose declared content is false, and the public path passes green}, because what
it sees is a certificate with a valid signature and a valid inclusion proof. Only
re-executing $J(\pi,T)$ recovers the truth, and taking that step away from the
verifier is exactly what the zero-knowledge design buys. The machine-checked
assertions are enumerated in \texttt{bench/results/baseline.md}; one row of that
file is worth repeating because it is the honest one: on
\emph{unforgeability} the baseline is \emph{not weaker} than the proof path, both
being Ed25519 and vkey binding, and we do not score a point for ZK there.

The asymmetry the table exposes is the one the signature baseline already
showed: the cheap arms require the verifier to re-run the judgment on plaintext,
while the private arms move that work into a proof or into a trusted party. PoP
is on the proof side, with a measured cost four to five orders of magnitude
above the signature arm and a trust root that, unlike the TEE arm, contains no
hardware.

\subsection{Issuance: detection, not prevention}
\label{sec:eval-issuance}

A provider that never issues a certificate is indistinguishable, from the
verifier's side, from a provider with nothing to certify; no cryptographic
layer forces issuance. What a transparency log changes is the \emph{detection}
rate, and only under an external obligation to append before delivery. In a
simulation with $N=2000$ deliveries, $m=100$ withheld violations and a sampling
rate $p=0.05$, an audit that samples the \emph{delivered} artifacts detects
$0.9948$ of the withheld set in exact arithmetic ($0.9940$ by Monte Carlo over
500 draws). An audit that samples the \emph{log} detects $0.0000$, because the
withheld deliveries have no log entry at all: the sampling frame is wrong, not
unlucky.

an external assumption, not a delivered property
(\S\ref{sec:disc-coverage}).
\subsection{End-to-end and auditing}
\label{sec:eval-e2e}

The end-to-end demo produces 14 certificates (streaming chain with early stop,
MCP argument and response checks, one zk proof) and anchors them; a third party
verifies with public artefacts alone, and the ledger chain, signature,

\texttt{passed} and the violated rule set row by row; a full re-run with real
\texttt{core} proofs gave 19/19 on both paths.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

Everything above was kept deliberately narrow, and this section says how narrow:
five places where a reader is most likely to round our claims up, followed by
the engineering limitations that remain.

\subsection{The caliber of the privacy claims}
\label{sec:disc-privacy}
\label{sec:disc-policy}

\textbf{``Content privacy'' means \emph{no plaintext of $T$ in the public
values}, not \emph{content is unrecoverable}.} Two independent reasons force
this reading---one at the proof-system level, one at the design level---and the
weaker reading is the one our own measurements support.

\emph{Proof-system level.} The default proving tier is SP1's \texttt{core}, and
SP1's published security model states explicitly that individual STARK proofs do
not currently satisfy the zero-knowledge property; the source agrees, with no
blinding anywhere in the proof stack and the shard proof carrying the trace
Merkle root and trace openings in the clear.%
\need{BLOCKER WP8 (citation): ``SP1's published security model states that
individual STARK proofs do not currently satisfy zero-knowledge'' is an external
assertion whose primary source has not been pinned to a citable section and URL.
If it cannot be verified, the sentence must be withdrawn and only our own
source-level audit retained.}
Soundness (G1) therefore holds
while the proof artefact itself does not hide the witness. The
\texttt{gr\-oth16} and \texttt{plonk} tiers wrap the inner STARK proof as a
private witness---the only mode in which hiding is even possible---but that is a
wrapper-level claim, it is not post-quantum, it needs $\ge 16$\,GB, and we did
not measure it (\S\ref{sec:eval-proofs}).

\emph{Design level, and the more fundamental one.} The public values
\texttt{response\_binding} and \texttt{response\_commitment} are both
\emph{public, offline-recomputable functions of $T$}. This is not an oversight:
\texttt{response\_binding} \emph{must} be publicly recomputable, because that is
how a verifier independently checks that the delivered $T'$ is the $T$ that was
proved---the swap-response defence. But the same property is a guess-and-verify
oracle for $T$.

It is worth being precise about the attack, because the obvious version of the
argument is wrong: ``a one-line response carries only $10^2$--$10^3$ bits of
entropy, so enumeration is feasible'' does \emph{not} follow---$2^{100}$ is as
unenumerable as $2^{256}$. The real attack is \emph{re-execution}
(Corollary \ref{cor:reexec}): the adversary's side information
(Assumption \ref{as:Z}) is the tuple (prompt, model, decoding parameters), not
the response space, so under greedy or low-temperature decoding $k \approx 0$
(Definition \ref{def:k}) and the attack costs \emph{one inference}, independent
of $|T|$. Since a verifier must independently recompute the binding, response
binding and response-content hiding are mutually exclusive.

We therefore restate (G2) as what is actually established: \emph{in private mode
the public values contain no plaintext of $T$}---which the leak experiment
confirms directly---and not as content irrecoverability. The consequence for the
comparison in \S\ref{sec:related} is that the ``content privacy'' cells on
\emph{both} sides must not be read as ``content is unrecoverable.'' This is a
bound on our own system first.

\emph{Policy privacy has the same caliber, for three different reasons.} The
salted commitment closes the public-value channel, but (i) the default
\texttt{core} artefact carries \texttt{spec\_canonical}, so it can leak the
policy and the salt together; (ii) L0/L1/L2 are certificate-layer projections,
and the circuit still writes the raw rule and kind into its public values; and
(iii) the measured inference curve of Table~\ref{tab:policy-infer} shows that
even the certificate layer is not all-or-nothing---L1 still leaks rule
categories. \emph{``Salt added'' is therefore not ``policy privacy solved,''}
and the word ``zero-knowledge'' in the title is a claim about the delivered
artefact only if the witness-hiding tier is delivered.

\subsection{Where zero-knowledge pays, and where it does not}
\label{sec:disc-zkmap}

Section \ref{sec:eval-baseline} measured the non-ZK alternative---a signed
Merkle log---and everything in it is milliseconds, so a proof has to earn its
cost against what a signature cannot do. The map has two axes: is the response
secret, and is the policy secret? Zero-knowledge has positive value only in the
corners where at least one of them is unknown to the verifier. In the transparent
corner---public response, public policy---a signed log certifies exactly the same
thing at three to four orders of magnitude less cost, and adding a proof is
redundancy rather than rigour. The familiar criticism that ``a signature would
have done'' is correct in that one corner and wrong in the other three; locating
the boundary is what turns the criticism into a measured design conclusion.

One dependency belongs in the open. The zero-knowledge property of a certificate
is a property of the \emph{wrapper}, not of the system as a whole, and the
default tier does not have it (\S\ref{sec:disc-privacy}). If \texttt{gr\-oth16}
does not become a delivery tier, we do not claim the artefact is
zero-knowledge, and the word must leave the title.

\subsection{Availability: the unanchored-pattern cost attack}
\label{sec:disc-dos}

A regular expression with an unanchored pattern---one with no left anchor, such
as \texttt{.*foo}---gives the matcher no way to reject a prefix of the response
early: every position stays a live start position until the whole string has
been read. The prover's work therefore scales with the response, and a party who
\emph{chooses} the response can drive it quadratically. In the agent setting
that party is often the agent itself, or whoever controls its output.

Two things are worth separating. This is a denial of service against the
\emph{prover}, not an attack on soundness: no false certificate is produced, and
the cost falls on the honest side. And it is specific to proving rather than to
matching, because the trace---not merely the verdict---is what gets proved.
Mitigations exist and are partly in place: a Pike VM (already implemented)
simulates the NFA in linear time, committing to the response length up front
removes the adversary's freedom to inflate it, and chunked matching bounds the
per-chunk work. Prior work on zero-knowledge regular expressions imposes the DFA
restriction but does not analyse denial of service in a zkVM setting.

\need{BLOCKER (author decision): whether to promote the $\Omega(n^2)$ claim to a
theorem. The proof is not written, so this draft states the cost as an
observation with the measured growth in Table~\ref{tab:dos}, not as a lower
bound. Promoting it requires a proof; demoting it permanently requires only
deleting this note.}

Two implementation defects surfaced when the matcher was differentially fuzzed
against Python's \texttt{re}. Both are now fixed, in commit \texttt{9ac2d59},
and we report them as closed rather than pending.
\textbf{G-1}: an empty character class such as
\texttt{[{\textasciicircum}\textbackslash d\textbackslash D]} used to compile to
an $\varepsilon$ edge and match the empty string, whereas Python treats it as
the empty language; the parser now rejects empty character classes at compile
time with \texttt{RegexSyntaxError}, so ``non-empty character class'' is a
parser contract rather than an extra assumption on the theorem.
\textbf{V1-2}: a zero-repeat pattern \texttt{/a\{0\}/} used to raise
\texttt{AttributeError}; it now compiles to an explicit $\varepsilon$ fragment,
and the Rust naive matcher's empty-text $\varepsilon$ gap was fixed at the same
time. The second change touches guest code, so the policy verification key moved
from \texttt{0x0091b406\ldots} to \texttt{0x003eb14d\ldots} and the new
\texttt{pop-script} binary is \texttt{sha256 7f99fdb1\ldots}; results produced
before the fix must not be mixed into a table that uses the new key. Neither
defect broke soundness---V1-2 failed before proving---and the fuzz campaign
reported zero mismatches on the supported subset, with the two expected-failure
entries converted to positive assertions.

\subsection{Coverage: a provider can simply withhold certificates}
\label{sec:disc-coverage}

Nothing in the cryptographic layer can compel a provider to issue a certificate
at all: a provider that never produces one is indistinguishable, from the
verifier's side, from a provider with nothing to certify. Without trusted
hardware there is no anchor that forces issuance, and the mechanisms that
could---sampling audits, economic penalties, disclosure obligations---live above
the cryptographic layer.

The experiment of \S\ref{sec:eval-issuance} makes the boundary precise rather
than rhetorical, and it has three parts. \emph{Impossibility}: if issuance is
optional, a provider that withholds violates completeness and no proof system
can detect it from the certificate alone. \emph{Conditional sufficiency}: if the
operator must append a digest binding the \emph{delivered} artifact before
delivery, and the log is append-only and publicly readable with inclusion
proofs, then issuance completeness holds for delivered-and-accepted artifacts
under those assumptions. \emph{Detection}: an audit over delivered artifacts
finds $0.9948$ of $100$ withheld violations at a $5\%$ sampling rate, while an
audit over log entries finds $0.0000$---the sample frame, not the sampling luck,
is the issue. Two limits travel with the numbers: we quantify \emph{detection},
never \emph{prevention} (penalties and incentives are outside the model), and
the log prototype implements only checkpoints, not consistency proofs or gossip,
so split-view resistance remains an external assumption rather than a delivered
property.

\subsection{Why composition alone does not close the gap}
\label{sec:disc-composition}

The natural next step is to compose: prove that the inference produced $T$ from
the request, conjoin that with a proof that $\pi(T)$ holds, and conclude that
the agent \emph{sent} a compliant response. The conclusion does not follow for
free.

\emph{Composition by itself does not close the gap.} A model proof bound to
\emph{some} input shows only that \emph{some} input yields a compliant output;
the divergent-delivery gap of Proposition \ref{thm:gap} closes only when the
model proof is bound to the \emph{input the user submitted}, $x$, so that
$M(x) = T \wedge \pi(T)$ becomes a statement about the bytes actually sent. That
requirement is not an extra condition bolted on: it is exactly $f > 0$ in
Theorem \ref{thm:t3}, with the user-submitted $x$ playing the role of the $f$
fresh bits. The two halves of this paper meet here---the request-side theorem
names the condition the composition would have to satisfy, and the response-side
theorem is what makes that condition non-vacuous. The resulting contract is
short: the inference proof must expose $T$ in its public values and must be bound
to $x$.

\begin{proposition}[Composition: five necessary conditions]
\label{thm:composition}
An accepted composite certificate implies
$\exists T: R(\pi,T)\wedge T=M(x_U)\wedge b=B(n,T)$ provided that
(C1) both halves are sound, with each delegated rule carrying a verified
companion proof; (C2) both halves are bound to the same fresh $n$;
(C3) the verifier recomputes $B(n,T_U)$ from the delivered $T_U$ and both halves
equal it; (C4) the inference half is bound to the submitted $x_U$;
and (C5) \texttt{delegated} is empty, or every delegated rule has a verified
companion proof whose public instance contains $\mathsf{encode}(T_U)$. Each
condition is necessary: dropping C2 admits two nonces, dropping C4 admits a
derived input, and dropping C5 admits an undecided delegated rule.
\end{proposition}

The four-cell experiment confirms necessity cell by cell
(Table~\ref{tab:compose}). Two obligations are \emph{not} discharged by
conjoining two valid proofs. First, the inference half must be bound to the
\emph{submitted} input. The current verifier binds it to
$\mathsf{derive}(T_U)$ instead, so it certifies only the weak form
$\exists x: x=\mathsf{derive}(T_U)\wedge T_U=M(x)$. Cell (c) is accepted with
\texttt{ok} and \texttt{satisfied} both true while the user submitted an input
different from $\mathsf{derive}(T_U)$. Closing it requires putting $x_U$ or a
commitment to it into the public values and recomputing $\mathsf{IB}(n,x_U)$
during verification; until then the strong form is not claimed. Second,
\texttt{delegated} must be empty or covered by a companion proof whose public
instance contains $\mathsf{encode}(T_U)$; cell (d) is accepted as a certificate
but fails the policy outcome, which is the intended fail-closed behaviour.
Composition therefore does not close the divergent-delivery gap by itself: it
closes it only when all five conditions hold simultaneously.

\need{BLOCKER WP4-(2)/WP3 (external resources): real-model scale
($\ge10^6$ parameters) and composition cost data are not measured. The claim
that composition cost is dominated by inference proving is \emph{unverified} at
proxy scale (\texttt{bench/results/compose.md}) and is not made here.}

\subsection{Gateway trust, and a blocked circuit check}
\label{sec:disc-gateway}

The tool-receipt chain is checked for structure inside the circuit, but its
signature is verified off circuit: Ed25519 inside the zkVM is expensive, and the
circuit type deliberately keeps the signature field out of its view. Two
consequences follow, and we state both rather than let the measured off-circuit
cost imply the other. First, the trust root still contains the gateway key: a
compromised gateway can sign receipts that do not match execution. The 2-of-3
threshold prototype weakens the single point of trust but does not remove it,
and it does not close the tail-truncation gap, because two gateways can still
collaborate on a truncated chain. Second, the in-circuit cost is
\emph{blocked}, not cheap: measuring it requires a new guest or a circuit
modification and a machine with enough memory to prove, neither of which this
study had. The cycle, proving-time and memory cells are therefore null. We do
not convert the $0.094$--$10.165$\,ms off-circuit figures into an in-circuit
estimate; the two carry different trust meanings and different work orders of
magnitude.

\subsection{Remaining limitations}
\label{sec:disc-rest}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Two delivery-side properties are assumed, not delivered.}
    In-circuit Ed25519 verification is blocked
    (\S\ref{sec:disc-gateway}), so the gateway remains part of the trust root;
    and the transparency log lacks consistency proofs and gossip, so split-view
    resistance is an external assumption (\S\ref{sec:disc-coverage}). Issuance
    results quantify detection only.
  \item \textbf{The policy compiler's two NFA defects are fixed, at the price of
    a new key.} G-1 (empty character class) now fails closed at parse time and
    V1-2 (zero-repeat) equals $\varepsilon$; both are closed in commit
    \texttt{9ac2d59}. The Rust-side fix changed the guest, so the policy vkey
    moved to \texttt{0x003eb14d\ldots} and the \texttt{pop-script} binary to
    \texttt{sha256 7f99fdb1\ldots}; earlier results are on the old key
    (\S\ref{sec:disc-dos}).
  \item \textbf{Policy privacy is conditional on the proving tier and the
    disclosure level.} The salt closes the public-value channel only; the
    \texttt{core} artefact and the circuit's raw rule fields remain open, and
    L1 still leaks rule categories (\S\ref{sec:disc-policy}).
  \item \textbf{Three-party collusion is undetectable at the signature layer.}
    If the policy author, the operator, and the prover all collude, the
    certificate is indistinguishable from an honest one. The multi-prover
    construction partitions responsibility; it does not prevent agreement
    (\texttt{docs/security-model.md}).
  \item \textbf{Signing and key custody are not production-grade.} The on-chain
    path currently takes plaintext private-key parameters, the demo uses a
    public Anvil test key, and deployment is a local Anvil instance. Production
    signing (Ed25519/HSM) and key custody remain future work.
  \item \textbf{Semantic-rule model quality.} The delegated semantic rule works
    end to end, but the bundled model is a demonstration proxy, not a usable
    learned guardrail: ``$P(\text{harmful})$ is low'' is a statement about
    \emph{this} model. Its public instance also contains $\mathsf{encode}(T)$, so
    semantic rules are \emph{public-mode only}.
  \item \textbf{The inference proof is faithful, not good.} The inference half
    is a deterministic fixed-point MLP proxy whose weights are generated from a
    compile-time seed and baked into the program, hence committed by the
    verification key. It proves that \emph{this} graph computed \emph{this}
    output on \emph{this} response---not that the graph is any good. Swapping it
    for a real prover touches only the prover; the composition layer does not
    move.
  \item \textbf{Verification remains $O(N)$.} There is no recursive aggregation,
    so a verifier pays per certificate. The supported regex subset and its ASCII
    semantics are bounded in the same way, and proof-cost optimisation (lookup
    arguments, parallelism, precomputation) is unexplored.
  \item \textbf{Slices share one guest.} Multiple provers hold distinct keys,
    but that is a division of responsibility rather than a per-role circuit: the
    repository does not build one circuit per role.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper began from a small question---what does a compliance certificate
actually bind?---and the answer came out the same on all three sides of the
interaction. A proof binds only the bits that were submitted into it \emph{and}
that the adversary could not select in advance. On the response side those bits
are the entropy $k_T$ of $T$ given everything the adversary already knows, and no
amount of computation buys more of them; on the policy side they are $k_\pi$ or
the freshness of a salt; on the request side they are the $f$ bits of freshness
the receiver contributes, and a certificate without them is replayable without
limit. Proposition~\ref{thm:t1}, Theorem~\ref{thm:dpriv} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:t3} are three faces of that one statement, and
Proposition~\ref{thm:gap}---a gap we state against our own design---is what
makes it bite.

PoP is the constructive instance of that analysis, not a claim to have invented
a new proof system. It composes an existing zkVM, an existing policy compiler,
and a signature layer into a certificate that a verifier holding only public
values can check without trusting hardware---and it is explicit about the three
things such a certificate does not do (\S\ref{sec:discussion}), which are limits
of the problem rather than of one implementation.

The design conclusions are accordingly modest, and we hope reusable: publish the
leak profile of a certificate and audit it like a disclosure policy; let a
privacy claim be only as strong as its arithmetic supports; and put the
freshness where the binding is. A certificate is worth exactly as much as the
bits that went into it.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{thm:t1}]
\emph{(1).} Fix $z$ and let the adversary's query set be
$Q = \{q_1, \dots, q_q\}$. Decompose by first hit,
\[
\Pr[\exists i : q_i = X] \;=\; \sum_i \Pr\!\left[q_i = X \wedge \neg E_{<i}\right],
\]
where $E_{<i} \triangleq \{\exists j < i : q_j = X\}$.
Conditioned on $\neg E_{<i}$, every oracle answer $a_1,\dots,a_{i-1}$ already
revealed is uniform and independent of $X$ (no queried point equals $X$, and $H$
is a fresh random function), and $\beta(X)$ itself is uniform and independent of
$X$. Hence $q_i$---a deterministic function of $(z, \beta(X), a_{<i})$---is
\emph{independent of $X$}, so
\begin{multline*}
\Pr[q_i = X \mid Z = z]
= \sum_x \Pr[q_i = x]\,\Pr[X = x \mid Z = z] \\
\le \max_x \Pr[X = x \mid Z = z] = 2^{-k_z},
\end{multline*}
giving $\Pr[\exists i : q_i = X \mid z] \le q\,2^{-k_z}$. Taking the expectation
over $z$ and using $\mathbb{E}_z[2^{-k_z}] = \mathsf{Guess}(X \mid Z) = 2^{-k_X}$
from Definition~\ref{def:k} yields $q \cdot 2^{-k_X}$. An adversary that outputs
an unqueried value wins with probability at most $2^{-k_z} \le q\,2^{-k_z}$,
which the same bound absorbs. No $q\cdot2^{-\lambda}$ term is needed in the
exact-recovery game: a query that lands on a different $x$ with
$\beta(x)=y$ is a second preimage of $H$, not a win. The explicit
$q\cdot2^{-\lambda}$ is kept only when the win condition is consistency with the
published $b$ rather than exact recovery, or when $q$ is unbounded.

\emph{(2).} Without an oracle for $\beta_s$, the value $b=H(s,X)$ is uniform and
independent of $X$ in the random-oracle model for an adversary that does not know
$s$, so the best strategy is the prior mode and succeeds with probability
$\mathsf{Guess}(X\mid Z)$. An attempted enumeration must also guess $s$, at cost
$2^{-\kappa}$ per query. The bound is therefore
$\mathsf{Guess}(X\mid Z)+\mathrm{negl}(\kappa)$, with no $q$-fold amplification.
Part (2) buys hiding only below the public-form threshold
$\log q+\log(1/\varepsilon)$; when $k_X=0$ the prior mode already recovers $X$
and no binding helps.

\emph{(3).} Public recomputability gives (1). If (1) exceeds $\varepsilon$, the
only way to avoid it is a binding that a verifier cannot recompute, i.e.\ a keyed
binding, which by part (2) forfeits PV-2. The third leg---hiding plus PV-2
without binding---is excluded from the class of compliance schemes because a
certificate whose binding does not pin $X$ certifies nothing about the delivered
artifact.
\end{proof}

\noindent\emph{Preimage-class bound.} For an arbitrary deterministic public
$\beta$, conditioning on $\beta(X)$ gives
$\Pr[\mathcal{A}^\beta(Z,\beta(X))=X]\le
\mathbb{E}_y[\max_x\Pr[X=x\mid Z,\beta(X)=y]]$, with no hypothesis on $\beta$.
For $\beta=\mathrm{id}$ or an injective $\mathsf{encode}$ this equals $1$; for a
lossy encoding it equals the inverse of the preimage-class size.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:dpriv}]
Fix an admissible tuple $(\pi_0,\pi_1,T,n)$ with $D(\pi_0)=D(\pi_1)$ and
$R(\pi_0,T)=R(\pi_1,T)$. Write $h_i=h_{\pi_i}$,
$R_i=L_{\mathrm{rest}}(\pi_i,T,n)$, $P_i=L_{\mathrm{proof}}(\pi_i,T,n)$ and
$\mathsf{Stmt}_i=(h_i,R_i)$. Consider the hybrids
\begin{align*}
H_0&=(h_0,R_0,P_0),\\
H_1&=(h_0,R_0,S_{\mathrm{proof}}(\mathsf{Stmt}_0)),\\
H_2&=(h_0,S_{\mathrm{rest}}(h_0,D,\mathrm{pub}_T,n),S_{\mathrm{proof}}(\mathsf{Stmt}_0)),\\
H_3&=(h_1,S_{\mathrm{rest}}(h_1,D,\mathrm{pub}_T,n),S_{\mathrm{proof}}(\mathsf{Stmt}_1)),\\
H_4&=(h_1,R_1,S_{\mathrm{proof}}(\mathsf{Stmt}_1)),\\
H_5&=(h_1,R_1,P_1).
\end{align*}
$H_0\approx H_1$ and $H_4\approx H_5$ cost $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{wh}}$ each by
C2; $H_1\approx H_2$ and $H_3\approx H_4$ cost
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pub}}$ each by C1. For $H_2\approx H_3$, split into
$H_{2a}=(h_1,S_{\mathrm{rest}}(h_1,D,\mathrm{pub}_T,n),S_{\mathrm{proof}}(\mathsf{Stmt}_0))$
and $H_{2b}=H_3$: $H_2\approx H_{2a}$ costs $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{com}}$ by C3,
and $H_{2a}\approx H_{2b}$ follows because
$\mathsf{Stmt}_0\approx_c\mathsf{Stmt}_1$ (C1 and C3 by triangle inequality) and
$S_{\mathrm{proof}}$ is a PPT function of the statement. The triangle inequality
gives $4\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pub}}+2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{wh}}+2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{com}}$,
which is $O(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pub}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{wh}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{com}})$.
The game fixes one $T$ because two different artifacts are distinguished by
$B(n,\cdot)$ itself, which is response-side leakage and not a policy advantage.
\end{proof}

\noindent\emph{Theorem~\ref{thm:leak}.} The proof is the first-hit decomposition
of Proposition~\ref{thm:t1}(1) with $(Z,\beta(X))$ replaced by the verifier view
$V$. The step that changes is the uniformity of answers to queries that have not
hit $X$: it holds conditionally on $L_{\mathrm{pub}}$ and fails when
$L_{\mathrm{proof}}$ is a function of the witness. A witness-hiding tier restores
$L_{\mathrm{proof}}=\bot$ and hence the public-value bound; a
\texttt{core} artefact does not, and the bound can reach $1$.

\noindent\emph{Theorem~\ref{thm:t3}.} The proof in \S\ref{sec:t3} is complete: a
union bound over the precomputed set controls replay, and a freshly minted
certificate that diverges yields two preimages of $b$ under $B(r,\cdot)$, which
is a challenge-binding failure. The $c$ factor is tight when the challenge sets
of the transcripts in $S$ are disjoint and of size $c$.

\need{BLOCKER (theorem-level, no external resource): the bounds are random-oracle
heuristics. Replacing the random oracle with a standard-model assumption for
SHA-256 would require a different proof, which is not available; the paper
therefore claims these bounds only in the random-oracle model and records the
SHA-256 instantiation loss in $\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)$.}

\section{Reproduction}
\label{app:repro}

The full walkthrough---environment, a single compliance proof, cross-checking
against the reference layer, private mode, certificate issuance, the one-command
demo, and third-party verification---is maintained in \texttt{docs/reproduce.md}
together with the exact commands and their expected output. This appendix records
only what a reader needs in order to judge the claims of \S\ref{sec:eval}.

\paragraph{Environment.} Ubuntu or WSL2 on x86-64, no GPU. The Rust/SP1 side
needs Rust stable, \texttt{cargo-prove}~v6.7.0, Go $\ge$\,1.24, and the usual
native toolchain; the reference layer is pure Python~3.10+ and runs on its own.
Memory, not time, is the binding constraint: the CPU prover carries a fixed
floor of approximately 10.15\,GiB regardless of the trace
(\S\ref{sec:eval-proofs}), and 12\,GB suffices only for small policies.

\paragraph{What reproduces without the prover.} The reference layer, the policy
compiler, and the whole test suite run in seconds and need no Rust. This is
enough to reproduce the non-ZK baseline of \S\ref{sec:eval-baseline}, to inspect
the certificate and inclusion proofs, and to check the cross-layer contract---%
that the Python reference implementation and the zkVM guest consume the same
compiled policy bytes.

\paragraph{What needs the prover.} Every timing, size, and memory figure in
\S\ref{sec:eval-cycles}--\S\ref{sec:eval-proofs}, and the end-to-end result of
\S\ref{sec:eval-e2e}, requires a real proof. The measurements were taken on a
24-core CPU machine with 12\,GB of RAM, which is what places the ceiling reported
there: the successes and the two out-of-memory configurations in
Table~\ref{tab:proofs} are both properties of that machine.

\paragraph{Where each number comes from.} Every measurement in \S\ref{sec:eval}
is the content of a checked-in result file with a machine-readable companion:
\texttt{bench/results/} holds \texttt{cycles}, \texttt{ablation},
\texttt{proofs}, \texttt{baseline}, \texttt{verify}, \texttt{semantic},
\texttt{streaming}, \texttt{compose}, and \texttt{prover\_knobs}, each as a
\texttt{.json} alongside its \texttt{.md}. The reproduction guide records the
commit, the hardware, and the prover binary digest for each run, so that a
reported figure can be traced to one specific execution rather than to a state
of the tree.
